The whole argument, in one line

1The business, in brief

A vertically integrated construction materials operation in New Town, North Dakota, on the Fort Berthold Reservation. It owns the whole sequence from raw material to delivered product: pit, crusher, batch plant, trucks. That is unusual for an operation this size, and it is the most important fact about it.

The structural position

Nearest ready-mix is 60 miles at Tioga, then 150 to 200 miles at Dickinson and Bowman. West Dakota Ready Mix in New Town is closed. Concrete has a 90-minute placement window, which makes distance decisive in a way it never is for gravel. A plant at New Town is the only practical option for most of Fort Berthold, and no competitor can answer it without building one here.

So the $3 million is what is left on the table in 2026, and it is material moving and invoiced inside the window, not work awarded now and delivered later.

A sanity check, because a reader will run one

$3M over roughly thirteen working weeks is about $230,000 a week. Against stated capacity of ~6,000 tons a week, thirteen weeks of aggregate alone at regional pricing of $22 to $26 a ton covers a large share of it, before ready-mix and topsoil, which earn considerably more per unit. The revenue and capacity figures corroborate each other. Both trace to the same source and neither is yard-confirmed, so consistency is reassuring rather than conclusive. Confirming capacity does double duty, since it substantiates the revenue figure too.

What the $3M still needs behind it

One number invites one question: made of what. Break it into named lines, each with who is buying, what material, how many tons, at what price, when it moves, and how firm it is. Five lines like that are worth more than one total and are much harder to argue with.

3The decision: why a website, and why now

One question was put plainly: can a buyer who has never met Richard confirm this operation is real, capable, and carrying what they need? Today the answer is no. No website, no published specifications, no price guidance, no materials list, no photographs, and effectively nothing findable for topsoil, gravel or concrete around New Town. A buyer who goes looking finds nothing, and so does a buyer who is given the name and goes to check.

The site does two jobs, on two different clocks

Verification — works day one

A buyer already wants to order and needs sign-off from a manager who has never met Richard. Or someone who heard the name looks before calling.

There is no indexing lag on a link somebody forwards. Live inside the current 90-day window. With the majors working on and around Fort Berthold, a buyer there placing gravel, topsoil or concrete is exactly this case.

It will not win those sales. Its absence can lose them.

Discovery — accrues over months

Being found by a buyer who has never heard of Concrete Warrior and is searching for a supplier.

Search engines and AI assistants need time to index a new site, accumulate its content and treat it as an established business rather than a new domain.

This return lands in 2027, and it is the half that argues for building through the shutdown.

Why now rather than in the spring

The ground freezes; purchasing does not. Budgets, bid preparation, annual supply arrangements and vendor selection for the coming season run right through the winter. The buyer told to find three gravel suppliers for next year's program does it in January and February, not the week the frost leaves. A site not findable until March misses the window in which the 2027 decisions get made. CONFIRM with Richard: when buyers here actually line up next season's material

The shutdown is also the only stretch with time to gather photographs, confirm specifications and settle prices. Standing this up in the spring means starting from zero in the same weeks the work returns.

What this document will not claim: that the website generates the $3 million. That business is already in motion and closes the way it always has, through Richard. The site protects deals rather than creating them, and the distinction matters because anyone in this industry will test it.

5Who the customers are, and how they buy

Business arrives three ways. The site does a different job in each.

1 · Direct trust

Knows Richard personally. Calls, gets a quote, done. No searching, no comparing.

Site: does not win this sale, the relationship did. But this buyer often needs sign-off from someone who has never met Richard, and the site is what gets forwarded up.

2 · Referred

Knows of Richard, or was given the name. Wants to look the company over before calling.

Site: credibility. Who owns it, what iron they run, what the yard looks like. This reader is deciding whether to call at all, and there is no second chance.

3 · Procurement, under instruction

Told by a boss: find three suppliers of topsoil, or concrete, or road gravel. Get quotes. Report back on what each offers and what it costs.

Site: everything. This is the case the build is for.

The buyer is often not the person who approves the purchase

Someone who knows Richard works for a major operator. They handle materials, they trust him, they want to place the order. But above a certain amount, or with a supplier the company has not used before, a manager has to sign off — a manager who has never met Richard and never will. They ask where the material is coming from, and the answer arrives as a link.

The site does not win that sale. Its absence can lose it.

At that moment the site is not marketing. It is evidence in an internal approval, read by someone deciding whether to let a subordinate commit company money to a supplier they have never heard of. That reader wants to verify, not be persuaded: a real business, real address, real equipment, plainly does this for a living.

A trusted relationship can still die in an approval queue. At large operators it compounds, because vendor onboarding expects a supplier to be independently verifiable, and a company with no findable presence can stall in setup before anyone has judged the material or the price.

So case 1 is not "the site does nothing." It is quiet work in the background of the largest revenue line, in a room Richard is not in. Nobody will report a deal that closed because of the website. It is simply one less reason for a manager to say no.

This is winnable, and the evidence is national

We graded 26 materials-supplier websites across the country, including the largest producers in the industry. Only two publish prices. Not one publishes what a given yard has in stock. Vulcan, the largest US aggregate producer, answers its product page with a contact form. The pattern holds at every size of company.

Being the only complete row costs nothing but publishing what Richard already knows. It does not require being the biggest supplier or the cheapest.

Confirm with Richard

These three cases are our working model, plausible enough to build on. One question decides how much weight case 3 carries: do operators buy material directly, or does their pad and road contractor buy it and bill it through? If it is the contractor, the buyer is a short list of local firms and the phone matters more than the search result. Simplest version: how did the last twenty orders actually arrive, and did anyone ever ask for a spec sheet?

6What we built, and why it looks the way it does

The standard this site should be held to

This is not a site selling luxury apartments, boutique coffee or a restaurant meal. Those sell atmosphere and are fairly judged on taste and polish. This one represents a working materials operation in western North Dakota, and it has a different job.

The honest measure is not whether the site looks impressive. It is whether Concrete Warrior was one of the three, and whether its row came back complete.

The decisions that follow

  • Specifications over atmosphere. Every material gets a real page: what it is, what it is for, the spec, how it is delivered. Not a paragraph and a phone number.
  • Real photographs, never stock. Stock gravel imagery reads as a broker rather than a producer, the one perception this business cannot afford.
  • Legibility over decoration. The reader is on a phone, in a truck, on poor signal, copying numbers into a spreadsheet.
  • Published numbers wherever the business will stand behind them. The largest competitive gap available, and it costs only the decision to publish.
  • A quote form that captures the whole question — material, quantity, delivery location and date, so Richard answers in one pass rather than three emails.
  • A standing asset, not a campaign. Prices and availability change; the structure holding them does not. Updated in an afternoon each spring rather than rebuilt.

Built to be found: three channels, not one

A supplier can be visible in one and invisible in the others, which is why they are treated as separate disciplines.

Search engines

  • Every material gets its own page, because that is how people search: road gravel, or Class 5, or ready-mix concrete, with a place attached.
  • Location in the content, not just the footer. A supplier's whole value here is being close.
  • Structured data on every page. A floor, not an advantage: it wins nothing by itself, and its absence quietly costs everything built on top of it.
  • Written in the buyer's words, not the company's. We recently audited a $191M paving and concrete contractor that ranks first for the language it uses about itself and does not appear at all for the plain phrase its customers type. Its pages were written in company voice.
  • Plus the unglamorous base: fast pages, correct titles and descriptions, clean internal linking, no conflicting signals.

AI assistants

A growing share of this research never reaches a results page. The buyer asks an assistant who supplies gravel near New Town and acts on the answer. Being absent from that answer is a new way to lose a sale, and almost nobody in this industry builds for it.

  • Answers written the way buyers ask, since assistants extract specific answers rather than brochure copy.
  • Published, specific facts. An assistant repeats only what it can find. "Contact us for details" gets left out of the answer entirely.
  • An llms.txt file with usage rules, including an instruction never to infer or fabricate a gradation, strength, sieve value or salinity number. On a site publishing specifications, an invented figure is a liability, not a marketing problem. Already in place.

The map, which here may outrank the website

A buyer on a job site searching for gravel near New Town sees map results before website results. Without a Google Business Profile, Concrete Warrior is not in that list at all, however good the site is.

A third clock, and the one to start first

A verified profile can surface in days or weeks, against months for organic search — the fastest findability asset available and the one most relevant to the current 90 days. But verification is the bottleneck and is genuinely unpredictable: postcard, phone, or a video walkthrough, and attempts fail and restart. We have a contractor client still unverified after months and several attempts.

If only one thing could be done this month, the profile would be a defensible choice over any single page of the website. Both are being done. It goes first because it has the longest lead time, the fastest payoff, and the highest chance of stalling on something outside our control.

  • One decision gates the filing. Staffed during posted hours means a standard listing with an address; nobody there means a service-area business with a delivery radius and no pin. Filing the wrong type is a common cause of suspension, and a suspension is far harder to undo than a correct filing is to make.
  • Entity standing is no longer an obstacle here. The registry now shows Concrete Warrior LLC active and in good standing, which removes what would have been a real complication in verifying a listing. See section 7.
  • Reviews are part of what the referred buyer sees. A competing local hauler carries 4.2 stars across 25 reviews; Concrete Warrior starts at zero.
  • The map already shows the gap. West Dakota Ready Mix in New Town reads as permanently closed. Buyers are being shown an empty slot with nothing filling it.
What the profile should be called

Recommended: "Concrete Warrior." The legal entity is Concrete Warrior LLC and there is no DBA, so this is the name that matches the company's own paperwork exactly. Google requires the profile to carry the real-world business name — what is on the sign, the invoices and the truck doors — and during verification it may ask for documentation. With the entity now in good standing, the legal name is the version that lines up cleanly with every document behind it, which is the fastest route through a process that is already the least predictable part of this project.

The domain does not need to match, and does not have to. Google has no such requirement. concretewarriorsupply.com remains the right web address for exactly the reason it was chosen: it presents the business more clearly online, and "supply" distinguishes it from Warrior LLC, an established Fort Berthold company holding TERO Level 1 preference. The website and the listing can carry slightly different forms of the name without conflict, which is ordinary and common.

The supplier signal comes from the categories, not the name, which is where it belongs anyway. Primary: ready-mix concrete supplier, the strongest structural position. Additional: crushed stone, sand and gravel, topsoil, and concrete supplier. A listing can hold several, and each is a separate way to be found. Adding materials or a town into the name itself ("Concrete Warrior Gravel & Topsoil New Town") is the most common cause of a listing being edited or suspended. Confirm exact category names in the Google interface; the taxonomy changes

One dependency, and it connects to the photographs. Verification is increasingly done by video walkthrough, in which Google asks to see signage. The listing name should match what the sign actually says. If the yard sign reads differently from the legal name, that is the version to file, so this is worth confirming once the photographs arrive. If there is no signage at all, that is worth knowing early, because it makes verification harder.

Under the hood

  • Domain secured, with privacy and auto-renewal.
  • Next.js on Vercel — fast by default, served globally, no plugin maintenance burden or plugin attack surface.
  • Every change version-controlled in a private repository. Reversible, and the site does not exist in only one place. A materials business should not be one hosting account away from losing its web presence.
  • An automated watcher checks the site daily and alerts if it goes down, breaks or shows signs of compromise. The failure it prevents is the common one: a small business site goes down and stays down, because the only person who would notice is a customer, and that customer just calls someone else.
  • Sitemap, Search Console and Analytics at launch. Search Console reports what people searched and clicked, the earliest signal available. Analytics reports which materials get read and where people leave. The point is to learn, not to report: the 2027 site should be better because a season of real data exists to shape it.
  • Indexing is currently switched off on purpose. This looks like an error and is not. An incomplete site indexed early makes a first impression that takes months to correct, so it stays off until the content, photographs and specifications are in, then is switched on deliberately.

7One item resolved, one still needing a decision

Both are facts anyone outside the business can check, which is why they are raised here rather than left to surface at a worse moment. The first has just been cleared.

1 · Entity standing — resolved, and verified

Five days ago the North Dakota registry showed Concrete Warrior LLC as Inactive, Involuntary, and not in good standing. Rechecked on 17 August, it now reads Active and in Good Standing (record 0006709316, filed 12 June 2024), with no reinstatement action outstanding. The paperwork went through.

This matters more than it might appear, and it is now a strength rather than a risk. Any buyer, lender or procurement officer can look the entity up in thirty seconds, and a growing number do it as routine vendor screening. They will now find a company in good standing. It also removes what would have been a genuine obstacle to verifying the Google listing, holding a license and signing agreements.

Verified directly against the state's FirstStop registry rather than a third-party directory, which caches stale status. Two Rhino Ready Mix records remain inactive, one of them voluntarily, which may well be deliberate and is a separate question from the operating entity. Worth one check: the customer-facing email is still a Gmail address carrying the Rhino name, while the entity in good standing now has a domain of its own.

2 · How this document describes the Texas relationship

The Patriot joint venture is a genuine asset. Concrete Warrior has no operating history of its own; Patriot has years of it on the same equipment and materials. That answers a question every new supplier gets asked.

But the wording carries risk in one direction. TERO reviews whether a qualified Indian contractor genuinely self-performs or passes work through to a non-Native entity. Describing Concrete Warrior as the northern arm of a Texas company is exactly the characterization that invites that scrutiny. Described accurately as a joint venture between two separate companies, it is an asset and nothing more. Get Richard's read before it is written either way.
