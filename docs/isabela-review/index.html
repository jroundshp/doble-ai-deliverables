Working Note · Version 2/ Isabela & Aguada, Puerto Rico/ 1 September 2026, revised/ Confidential

Isabela Quarry: what we read, what came back, and what is still open

The first version of this note set out what the document set established and closed with 28 numbered questions. Those questions came back answered the same day, in a nineteen-page response with two addenda and a quarterly MSHA series pulled fresh from the federal database. This version folds that in. It is still not a valuation and not a recommendation. Every change from the first pass is listed in section Zero, so that nothing has been quietly revised.

Please read this part first

Everything below is drawn from documents, and from nothing else. We have not visited the site, spoken with the seller or the accountants, or contacted the permitting agencies.

That mattered more than we expected. When we wrote the first version we flagged several things as missing or contradictory, and on four of them the document that settles it was already in the folder and we had not opened it, or had worked from a summary of it. Those four are listed by name in section Zero. The general lesson holds and is worth restating: where something here looks unsupported, the likeliest explanation remains a document we have not read rather than a fact that does not exist.

So if you have anything we have not seen, please send it. We would rather revise this note than defend it, and the same applies to anything we have simply read wrong. Corrections are more useful to us than agreement.

What changed since the first pass

Set out in full, because a revised document that does not show its own revisions is worth less than the first draft. Three findings are new and go to the heart of the case. Four things we had wrong. Eleven questions moved. Six things did not move at all.

Three findings that are new, and material

1. The one market comment on this rock says it is not highway grade DOC

FirstBank's appraiser, writing about the competitive set, put it this way: "Gutierrez polishing grade (required by the US Highway) of its material is excellent for road construction while the fine material or talc type material of Mundo Real is excellent for fine concrete finishes and bonds."

Read plainly, the quarry next door has rock that qualifies for federal-aid highway work and this one is characterised as a fines and concrete-finish material. That bears directly on the two largest pieces of the upside case: the 1,700 t/day asphalt plant, and bidding the western Puerto Rico infrastructure programme. It is one sentence in a market section, not a laboratory finding, and it is not conclusive. But it is the only independent comment on product grade anywhere in the set, and it points the wrong way.

First pass: did not surface it. The sentence sits in a file we had extracted and searched, on a page we had not read.

2. Nobody has ever drilled either site JL

The 14,191,213.95 m³ Arenales figure is a surveyed boundary volume — the geometry between the permit limits and the depth cap — not a drilled or sampled reserve. Cotto has no reserve document of any kind beyond the seller's letter. There are no boring logs, no core, no geologist's report, nothing on bedding, overburden, clay-filled karst pockets or voids. In karst limestone the distance between boundary volume and recoverable saleable tonnage can be very large.

Nor has the rock ever been qualified for the uses the projections assume. There is no Los Angeles abrasion result, no sulfate soundness, no ASTM C33 gradation, absorption or specific gravity. The single laboratory result in the entire set is an agricultural-lime analysis.

The recommendation attached to that finding is a minimum four-hole core programme to 75 feet at each site, eight holes in total, laid out by a geologist, logged and photographed, with samples sent for abrasion, soundness, gradation, absorption and alkali–silica reactivity. A quotation is being sought from a geotechnical firm on the island. Counsel's draft already conditions closing on a geologist's reserve study without defining its scope; this is the scope.

First pass: we treated the reserve question as resolved, because the 14.2M and 5.2M figures reconcile as permit boundary against extraction plan. They do reconcile. The point we missed is that neither number has ever been tested against rock.

3. The Aguada land may not belong to the company being sold DOC

Two separate property records are in play at Aguada, and the first pass read them as one bad measurement. They are two parcels. The Registry parcel — finca 4,490, 10,032 m², 2.55 cuerdas — is registered to Hormigonera Chaparro, Inc., and is the parcel the 2021 bank appraisal actually valued. The CRIM cadastral record that the deal documents use — 068-100-363-14-000, 6.64 acres — carries a last sale of $67,100 on 8 October 2004 and an owner of record named Héctor Chaparro Carrero, who is not a party to the purchase agreement. That record is roughly 16,800 m² larger than the titled parcel.

The likeliest explanation is a CRIM grouping error, which is an administrative correction rather than a title defect, and the 2021 appraiser flagged the cadastral mismatch himself at the time. But it cannot be assumed. If the plant, the silos or the paving sit partly on the land that is not finca 4,490, that land belongs to someone else. Roughly 60% of the appraised improvement value at Aguada is pavement. The title study needs to map the improvements onto the finca boundary before anything else at that site is priced.

First pass: listed "2.55 cuerdas on one document and 6.64 acres on another" as a housekeeping item for counsel, under acreage rectification. It is not housekeeping.

Four things we had wrong

The twenty-fold land gap at Chaparro was a category error. Withdrawn. DOC

We set the seller's $3M for Chaparro against a $150,000 appraised land value and said the one line testable against an appraisal failed by twenty times. The seller's own letter says the $3M covers "land, machinery, equipment, permits" and adds that "permits represent the most significant value component." It was never a land figure. Set against $850,000 of appraised land and site improvements, the question is whether about $2.15M of net equipment and permit value sits on top, which is an equipment-appraisal question and a reasonable one.

What survives, and is narrower but real: Hormigonera Chaparro's own balance sheet carries $3,100,242 of land, unchanged and undepreciated across 2020–2024, on a 2.5-acre parcel a bank appraiser valued at $150,482. Nothing in the set supports it. That is now question 17.

The $6.1M headline needs qualifying DOC

The total stands. What we did not do is read the appraisal's own components, which the appraiser gave in full: real estate $3,475,000 at $41,500 per cuerda, machinery and equipment $1,500,000, and about $275,000 of residual intangibles, making the $5,250,000 going concern. Real estate plus equipment alone is $4,975,000, which is close to the seller's $4.5M+ for the quarry entity rather than far from it.

The appraiser also split the going concern by sales comparison on a page the data-room copy dropped: the Isabela batch plant is valued at $1,232,000 on EBITDA of $176,000 against the seller's ~$2M, and the quarry alone is implied at $4.0M to $5.05M depending on method. So the seller's schedule is not unmoored from the appraised parts. The gap that remains is the honest one: no third party has ever valued the 219-acre Cotto reserve, and roughly $8.9M of the $15M has nothing behind it but that parcel and the growth case.

The assay was legible, and it was in the folder DOC

We reported the limestone analysis as a scan with no readable text and said the 94.6% figure traced only to the seller's materials. It is a third-party result: American Agricultural Laboratory, McCook, Nebraska, lab number 1728, reported 19 June 2023 for Empresas Mundo Real. CCE 94.6%, calcium 38.42%, magnesium 0.19%, ECC 67.1%, 51.7% finer than 60 mesh, moisture 1.7%.

The finding that replaces it is more useful than the one we had. It is an agricultural lime test. It supports the ag-lime product line and says nothing whatever about construction aggregate. The product label in the data room claims only 80% calcium carbonate, so the seller is understating on the bag. See new finding 1 above.

And the record that had been sitting there all along

The mine's federal safety record is good, and it is now in hand DOC

Nineteen years of MSHA enforcement history for Mine ID 54-00447: 111 routine citations and four training withdrawal orders, and no failure-to-abate, unwarrantable-failure, pattern or imminent-danger action of any kind. Not on the Pattern of Violations list. Zero significant-and-substantial citations in the last twelve months. Violations per inspection day 0.62 against a national surface metal and non-metal average of 0.94. Both required inspections every year.

Zero fatalities across 305,039 operator-hours from 2012 to 2024. Four reportable injuries in ten years, all days-away-only, three of them in the maintenance workshop. No haul-road, highwall or crusher incidents. Fifty-four industrial-hygiene sampling events since 2017: dust, silica and welding fume all well below the permissible limit. Noise at the crusher-operator and welder positions exceeded the action level repeatedly and the permissible limit twice, and MSHA accepted the hearing-conservation programme as the control.

Two honest qualifications. On a straight rate basis the injury frequency is not below the national average: four lost-time cases over roughly 330,000 hours is about 2.4 to 2.6 per 200,000 hours against a national surface figure of 1.1 to 1.7. With four events the comparison carries little statistical weight, and the severity profile is genuinely at the floor. Both things are true and the memo should say so rather than adopt the investor report's framing. Second, that report describes a 28-person quarry; MSHA's own numbers say 21 to 23, and so does the company's roster.

What is open: about $3,500 of unpaid penalties — $1,225 from eight citations issued between March and October 2025, four now delinquent and four referred to Treasury, plus a $2,276 residual on a 2007 assessment; an October 2025 training withdrawal order for an inadequately documented new miner; and two 2025 citations for the quarterly employment report itself, which makes Part 50 reporting a live compliance gap. All of it is cheap to clear and all of it belongs in the closing conditions.

What did not change

  • Nothing is executed. All four legal documents still carry a not-for-execution legend. The only signed item is the one-page letter of intent with the seller's signature block blank.
  • The letter of intent's sixty-day funding undertaking still runs to about 18 September 2026, which is now roughly seventeen days away. Nothing in the response bears on it, and the drilling programme it recommends takes several weeks. Those two facts are in direct tension and somebody has to decide which one gives.
  • Whether the roughly $6.5M of bank and SBA debt sits inside or on top of the $15M is still unagreed, and the interest rate on the $14M note is still blank. Counsel's description of it as the single largest open economic term stands.
  • Excluding Arenales from the seller's collateral remains the highest-value term in the document. Permits follow the parcels, so a foreclosure on Arenales takes the extraction permit and the aggregate supply for both concrete plants and the proposed asphalt plant with it.
  • There is still no general ledger, trial balance, bank statement or accounting export anywhere. Every statement remains compilation-basis with no opinion or assurance expressed.
  • There is still no NDA between buyers and sellers, and the data room still holds unredacted Social Security numbers and payroll data. That is live exposure today, irrespective of whether the deal proceeds, and it is the one item on this page that should be dealt with this week whatever else happens.

Two things the 2024 appraisal says that bear on price DOC

It is a full USPAP report prepared for a bank weighing its own loan exposure, not for a sale, which makes it the only independent valuation in the transaction. The $5.25M is an EBITDA of $750,000 times a multiple of 7.0, and that projection assumed quarry sales of $2.0M. EMR's actual 2024 sales came in at $1,597,692, about 20% under the figure the appraisal was built on.

The appraiser also valued the same property on 10 November 2022 and records that the going-concern value fell between the two dates, because the sales increase the earlier appraisal assumed never materialized. The whole valuation is stated under an express extraordinary assumption that the property holds all required permits and is free of environmental problems. No environmental report was available to him.

$6.1M is not a valuation of this deal. It is the sum of the parts anyone has actually valued, and it leaves out Chaparro's going concern and the entire 219-acre Cotto reserve. That gap is the honest bull case, and it is also question 16 below.

Two things have changed underneath it. First, question 18 is answered and the answer is no: the extraction permit caps depth at 66 metres above sea level or one metre above the bordering road, so 3.0M m³ of the company's own stated plan — more than half of it — lies outside the permit it currently holds. Going below the road is a permit modification, with a process and a timetable, not an operating decision a new owner makes.

Second, and larger: the 14.19M m³ is a surveyed boundary volume, not a drilled reserve. It is the geometry between the permit limits and the depth cap. Five

What is now on the critical path

The first version ended with questions. This one ends with a sequencing problem, because two of the things the response recommends take longer than the only clock in the transaction.

The drilling programme and the funding window are in direct conflict

The letter of intent's undertaking to fund $1,000,000 runs to about 18 September 2026, roughly seventeen days out. A core programme — eight holes to 75 feet, logged and photographed, with samples out for abrasion, soundness, gradation, absorption and alkali–silica reactivity — takes several weeks from authorisation to laboratory report, and that is before a geologist converts boundary volume into recoverable spec-grade tonnage.

Those two facts cannot both be honoured. Either the programme is authorised now and runs in parallel while the funding conversation continues, or the window passes and is renegotiated, or the deal is signed on rock nobody has tested. That is a decision for the buyer group this week and it should be made deliberately rather than by default. The letter of intent carries no exclusivity, no deposit and no stated expiry, which makes renegotiating it cheaper than it looks.

The cost asymmetry is worth stating plainly. Eight core holes and a laboratory suite is a small number set against a $15,000,000 price, and against a $14,000,000 seller note whose repayment depends entirely on tonnage this quarry has never been shown to be able to sell into the markets the projections assume.

The title study at Aguada. Registry certifications for finca 4,490 and finca 11,771, with the improvements mapped onto the finca boundary, will establish whether the plant, silos and paving stand on land the selling company owns. The original appraisal from FirstBank. The data-room copy has lost its embedded images, including the 2016–2023 EBITDA table the appraiser announces on page 50 and the site plan on page 36. The bank has the original, and that table would answer the utilisation and base-year questions at once.
