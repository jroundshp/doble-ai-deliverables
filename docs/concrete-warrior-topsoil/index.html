Before the selling

Screen what has been ordered

The biggest lever on this page is not a sales call. It is how much more gets screened before the buyers show up.

A yard of screened topsoil with no buyer has already used its fuel, equipment hours and wear. Some costs, like the crew, run whether the screens do or not. The ones that follow the screens do not, and none of them comes back until the dirt is sold. The workbook raises the same idea near the bottom of its Volume Plan: match screening to booked shipments unless there is an obligation to clear the source. Stockpiled topsoil still has value. It is next spring's inventory, and it can be sold off the pile after the trommels stop in November. But it will not pay anybody in October.

So the question that decides this is operational rather than commercial: does the ground have to be cleared by a certain date? If it does, screening continues and the pile is a financing question to plan for now. If it does not, running the screens at the pace orders arrive saves that spending for dirt somebody has already agreed to buy.

The buyer this workbook leaves out

Reclamation still matters most

The workbook sets aside the oil-well reclamation list on purpose, which makes sense for a list of non-oil buyers. For the strategy as a whole, though, the timing matters. The state's native grass specification allows a dormant fall seeding after October 20, so the one delivery season for reclamation topsoil opens in five weeks, inside your eight-week horizon. Production stops around mid-November, but the pile can keep delivering for a couple of weeks after that.

Chord's New Town field office got a purchasing voicemail on 16 September. That is worth a walk-in rather than a second voicemail. The operators themselves mostly buy through ISNetworld, Veriforce or Avetta, which Concrete Warrior is not yet registered on and which takes at least thirty days. The reclamation contractors who order dirt by the load are on the tracker already and can be called now.

Price

Quote the price delivered

A buyer compares what the dirt costs once it's on their site, not what it costs at the pit. The workbook already has the numbers for this. At its planning rates of $150 per truck-hour and 20 yards per load, each extra hour on a truck's round trip adds $7.50 a yard. These are the workbook's planning assumptions, not trucking quotes.

Keeping score

One comparison, every Friday

Yards ordered against yards screened. The calls are already being logged in Salesforce, so the yards each account has committed to can go on the same record. Our suggestion: if orders are not closing the gap by the end of September, that is the signal to slow the screens further. If they are, the screens can speed back up and the pile turns into cash.

What only you can answer

Four questions

These shape the strategy more than any research

Corey, Richard and Jeff · answer in a conversation

  1. How much topsoil has left the yard so far? Richard's log recorded the first pile finished at 27,300 yards processed on 10 September. With 35,000 on hand today, the answer tells us whether the pile is growing or turning over.
  2. Does the ground have to be cleared by a certain date? This decides whether screening can follow orders or has to run regardless.
  3. Has the pile been lab-tested or certified, and by whom? Oil and public buyers will ask on the first call.
  4. What is the lowest price you would take from a wholesale buyer who collects 5,000 yards or more? The wholesale calls can't close without it.
