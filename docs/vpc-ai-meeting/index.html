Quoting and submittals: where we start

A working document for the September 9 session. Most of this page is questions, and the answers are in the room rather than here.

What this hour is for

Not a presentation, and nothing here needs a decision at the end of it. VPC is a hybrid shop. Some of what it sells configures from families it already builds, flumes, manholes, standard enclosures and the EasyRec line, and much of the rest is engineer-to-order, made to a drawing. Either way the job passes through a stretch of digital work before anyone touches material: the inbound request, the missing dimensions, the first drawing, the price. The plan is to spend this hour on how that actually works today. What we have written down from outside the building is a guess, and the useful part of the next sixty minutes is the people in this room correcting it.

01 The lane

Everything between a request arriving and a packet going back out. The marketing and findability work is the other half of the same program and it runs alongside this one. It is not what this hour is about.

One question from that other half is worth two minutes at the end rather than a share of the hour: which vertical should be easier to find first, municipal and plant FRP or InterActive Play. That is a sequencing question and the answer to it is yours.

The part worth saying first

This does not replace anyone, and the first real deliverable is not a piece of software. It is a written map of how VPC actually quotes, which today lives in the heads of the people who price by hand. Even if the drafting never gets good enough to trust, that map is worth having, and it is the reason the first step carries so little risk.

02 What we think we know, and where it probably breaks

All of this came from VPC's public pages and catalog. None of it came from inside the building, so treat every line as a guess to be corrected.

Two shapes of job, not one

VPC looks like a hybrid shop, not a pure catalog and not pure custom. On the municipal water and wastewater side there are product families that can be sized and priced inside known ranges: flumes, manholes, standard enclosures, weirs and baffles. Alongside them sits work that is engineer-to-order and built to drawing: shelters and buildings, duct, fans, odd plant hardware. The aquatic side appears to split the same way, with EasyRec closer to a product line and InterActive Play quoted per job as project design.

If that split is roughly right, the two shapes want different help, and only the second one is graded against closed jobs.

The rest of the guesswork

  • Requests arrive in wildly different forms, and a meaningful share are missing something needed to price.
  • Chasing the missing information is a real cost and probably an unmeasured one.
  • The published packages already imply an option tree: window, heat, exhaust, light, chlorination package, voltage, load center.
  • Inside either shape, some families repeat far more than others, and the repeating ones are where a draft could be graded.
  • The example we started from is small: six feet by three feet, bathroom, 220 volt, window. There is no such SKU, which is exactly why it is a useful test. It sits inside a range VPC already builds, and somebody here already knows how to price it by hand.

03 The questions

This is the meat of the hour.

  1. Roughly what share of revenue is price-list or configure, against engineer-to-order? A rough split is fine, and a disagreement in the room about it is more useful than a tidy number. It decides which of the two shapes a first test lives in, and it is not the same question as which family clogs the queue.
  2. Who takes the inbound request today, and what happens to it in the first hour?
  3. Which product family clogs the queue? Not the most interesting one. The one that backs things up.
  4. Where does a job actually die?
  1. Of the pricing requests that come in, what share turn into sales? And is that tracked anywhere, or is it a feel? Either answer is useful. If the number does not exist, that is worth knowing before anything else gets built.
  2. What does a complete request look like, next to what usually arrives instead?
  3. What already gets reused between one quote and the next, and what gets rebuilt every time even though it probably should not?
  4. What would a first draft have to get right before it saved you time rather than costing you time?
  5. Where does the current process work well enough that touching it would be a mistake?

One complication worth putting on the table

The family clogging the queue and the family best suited to a first test may not be the same family. A backup can come from the most bespoke work in the shop, and that is the worst possible place to grade a first draft. If those two answers diverge in this conversation, that is a useful result rather than a problem, and it changes where we start.

04 What we would ask for at the end

Deliberately small, and smaller than the process behind it makes it sound.

  • Two closed jobs on whichever family comes out of question 3: the inbound file as it arrived, the hours, the drawing, the price, and what changed between the first packet and the final one.
  • Two hours with whoever owns the quote packet day to day.

The reconstruction itself is ours to do, not yours. What we are asking for is access to two files and somebody's time, not a package assembled for us in advance. Keeping it this small is the point: it should cost the person who owns the quote packet an afternoon, and if it costs more than that we have asked for the wrong thing. It is also the only homework this hour produces, and it comes after the questions rather than before them.

Two jobs and two hours is enough to learn whether the records reconstruct at all, whether the family in question is the one actually clogging the queue, and whether there is anybody here who wants this. If they come back clean, the ten jobs and the week are worth asking for, and that ask arrives with evidence behind it. If not, we found out in an afternoon instead of at month two.

Worth knowing in advance what tends to be missing from a reconstruction: the price and the final drawing are almost always there, while the hours attributed to a job and the inbound file exactly as it arrived often are not. Those two are the ones to check first.

05 Before any file moves

These are closed jobs for municipal utilities, industrial plants, the Navy and the Coast Guard, which makes the drawings the customer's business as much as VPC's. The condition comes before the request, not after it. Files stay inside VPC's control. Nothing goes into a public model or into anyone's training data. How they are handled gets written down and agreed before the first one moves, and the first document to read is not our policy but VPC's own contract terms with those customers, because some government and municipal agreements dictate how drawings may be stored and who is allowed to see them. If a family's paperwork cannot clear that, the test moves to a family whose paperwork can.

06 What happens after this

Nothing gets built out of this meeting. If the two jobs come back and the two hours happen, the next session is a review of the written process and how a first draft scored against work that already shipped, graded by the people who priced it originally. Nothing goes to a customer until that clears.
