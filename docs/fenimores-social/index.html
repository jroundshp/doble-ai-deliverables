Photos from the floor to Facebook and Instagram

A question came off the floor on the 9 September call: the staff are taking the pictures, so how do those pictures get posted the same day rather than sitting in an inbox? This is the answer. It comes in two layers, a calendar built well ahead that publishes itself and a same-day layer on top of it, and in three stages. The first stage needs no new software and can start this week.

For Bob, Brooks, Tammy, Katie and Corey. Working draft, not a public page.
This document is about one thing only: the path a photograph travels between the moment it is taken and the moment it appears on @fenimorestable on both platforms. It does not move an account, change a login, or ask anyone to hand anything over.
Companion pages: private events and the two pages, where the website stands, and the Cooperstown index.

01

What was asked

Good photographs are already being taken at Fenimore's. The drinks set that went round recently is the example everyone had in mind. What is missing is the next step: once a picture exists, the path it takes is not written down anywhere, so it depends on someone remembering.

Three specific things came up in that stretch of the call, and all three are fixable:

  • The photo has no destination. It gets emailed to several people at once, which reads to each of them as somebody else's job.
  • The hour matters and is currently accidental. A breakfast plate earns its keep at 7:40 in the morning. The same photograph at nine at night is decoration.
  • Social carries real weight in Cooperstown, particularly with the younger crowd, so the gap between taking a picture and posting it is costing actual covers.

All three come down to the same thing. The path is too long and nobody's name is written on it.

02

One rule before anything else: the pictures stay real

Cooperstown has a low tolerance for posts that read as machine-made, and that was said plainly on the call. A post that looks generated invites the reply why would I go somewhere that isn't real, and that is a hard thing to walk back in a town this size.

So the rule for everything below: if nobody at Fenimore's held a camera, it does not post. Every image is a real photograph of real food, real drinks, the real room, the real staff. No generated pictures, no generated people, no stock.

What software is genuinely good for here is the plumbing, and only the plumbing: the reminder, the hour, the crop, and a first draft of a caption written from a sentence a person actually said. The picture and the voice stay human. In a town of roughly 2,500 people, where the audience recognises the room and the staff in the photograph, that constraint is worth more than it costs.

03

Capture and publishing are two different jobs

At the moment they travel together. The photograph and the decision to post it move as a single email, which means a picture can only move as fast as whoever opens that email. Separate them and the whole thing loosens up.

  • Cooperstown owns capture. The people standing next to the food take the pictures and write the line that goes with them. That is already happening and it is the half that cannot be done from anywhere else.
  • The accounts stay exactly where they are. Nothing in this document requires moving @fenimorestable or changing who administers it.
  • One name owns the push for a given stretch of the week. A single person, written where the staff can read it, rather than a group in which everyone can reasonably assume it is covered.
  • Nothing in the daily rhythm waits on an approval. A photograph of today's lunch special does not need a review cycle. The pictures that carry the room and the pictures tied to an event or a price are different categories and should move at different speeds.

That last point is the one that actually does the work. A photograph sent to five people has been sent to nobody, and no software fixes that. A photograph sent to one named person on a Saturday afternoon gets posted on a Saturday afternoon.

04

How the person on the floor actually gets to post

Neither of the two things people usually assume. Not a shared username and password, and not full administrator rights either.

Nobody should be handed a password. A shared login cannot be withdrawn from one person without changing it for everyone, it makes the activity log useless because every post came from the same account, and it breaks two-factor authentication, which is the thing standing between the accounts and someone taking them. It also creates a genuinely awkward moment the day somebody leaves.

What happens instead: each person signs in as themselves, and someone who already has full control grants them a role on the Facebook Page and on the linked Instagram account. The password never moves. This is done from Meta Business Suite, under the people or access settings for the Page.

The level they need is the small one. Enough to create a post, schedule it, and reply to comments and messages. Not settings, not billing, not advertising, and not the ability to add or remove other people. Meta describes this as task access or partial access depending on which panel you land in, and they rename these screens often enough that whoever does the granting should read the labels in front of them rather than work from anyone's screenshot, including ours.

Two conditions have to be true first, and neither is visible to us from outside. The Instagram account has to be a professional account, business or creator rather than personal, and it has to be linked to the Facebook Page. If either is missing, the roles and the scheduling simply do not exist as options and the screens will look broken to whoever is trying to follow instructions. Somebody with access should confirm both before anyone is added.

The objection that comes up every time, worth answering before it is asked: being added to a business Page gives the business nothing at all on that person's private profile. It does not post to their profile, their friends cannot see it, and the only visible difference on their phone is a switcher for moving between themselves and the restaurant. Staff turn this down for a reason that is not true, so say it out loud when you ask them.

Grant it to both of the names in §08 rather than one, and keep full control with at least two people. A single administrator is fine right up until the Saturday they are unreachable.

05

What a finished post is actually made of

Four pieces, and the fourth one behaves completely differently on the two platforms, which is the part that gets missed.

  1. The photograph

    Real, per §02. One good one beats four.

  2. The caption

    What it is, and a reason to come now rather than at some point. The sentence written by whoever took the picture is the raw material for this.

  3. The tag

    The location tag first, because that is how a visitor searching Cooperstown finds the room at all, and it is currently the cheapest thing on this list to start doing. Then the sister accounts where the post genuinely involves them, and any person or supplier actually in the shot. A handful of hashtags, not thirty.

  4. The link, which is where the platforms split

    On Facebook a URL in the post text is tappable and goes where it says. On Instagram it is not. A link in an Instagram caption renders as plain text that nobody can tap, so the caption has to send people to the profile instead, and the profile link has to be worth arriving at.

Whether there is a link in it, and where that link points, is not visible to us without an account. If the strategy is to point Instagram at the bio, then the bio link is load-bearing and somebody should look at what it currently does.

07

Two layers: a calendar built well ahead, and a same-day layer on top of it

Everything above describes the same-day layer, which is the half that has to happen in Cooperstown because that is where the camera is. It is only half. Underneath it belongs a planned calendar, built in advance and scheduled to publish on its own, and that half is better done anywhere except on the floor during service.

The backbone: built ahead, scheduled once, publishes itself

A surprising amount of the year is known months out. None of it needs to be written on the morning it goes out, and the office that already does the design work is the right place to build it, on an ordinary weekday, with time to make it good. On the call the schedule was described as being worked about a month in advance, so this is not a new idea so much as an existing one worth formalising and then extending.

What belongs on it, and most of these can be written in one sitting for a whole season:

  • The football calendar. Monday night, Thursday night, Sunday afternoons, the college Saturdays. Every one of those dates is already published by the leagues, and each is a reason to post that is known in August. Ties directly to the screens and the EverPass question from the same call.
  • The holidays that move covers. Thanksgiving, Christmas Eve, New Year's Eve, Valentine's Day, Easter, Mother's Day. These need lead time rather than same-day inspiration, because people book them a week or three ahead, which means the post has to run before the week they are deciding.
  • The dates the town runs on. Induction weekend, the tournament weeks, the shoulder weeks either side when Cooperstown empties out and a breakfast message is worth more than a dinner one. Whoever knows the town fills in the actual dates. We would be guessing.
  • Anything already booked. Private events, live music, a menu changing over, a change in hours. These come from Cooperstown and go onto the same calendar rather than into a separate document.
  • The standing weekly beats. If breakfast is the thing nobody knows about, it earns a recurring slot rather than depending on someone remembering to photograph an omelette.

Then schedule it, so it publishes without anybody being available. That is the actual point. A post scheduled on a Tuesday in September goes out on Thanksgiving morning whether or not a single person is near a phone, and the same is true of a Sunday kickoff or a New Year's Eve service. Every argument in this document about who is around at which hour disappears for the planned layer, because nobody has to be around at all.

The same-day layer sits in the gaps

The photographs then fill in around the backbone rather than carrying the whole load. That is a much smaller ask of the floor, and a much more realistic one: nobody has to invent a posting programme mid-service, they just have to notice a good plate and send it.

So anyone who can post has to be able to see what is already scheduled. Without that, two breakfast posts land ninety minutes apart, or a drinks photo goes up on top of an events push that took a fortnight to arrange. That is not a discipline problem, it is a visibility problem, and it resolves the moment the schedule is something people can look at.

The useful part: the calendar is already paid for. Meta Business Suite has a planner showing everything scheduled across the Facebook Page and the Instagram account on one view, and the task-level access in §04 includes seeing it. So this is not a tool to build or buy. It is a screen that already exists, and it becomes visible to the floor the same afternoon the access is granted.

Three conventions make the two layers work together, and they are worth agreeing out loud rather than assuming:

  • The scheduled posts are the backbone and the daily photo fills the gaps between them. The photograph is opportunistic by design, which is its value and also the reason it yields.
  • Look at the planner before posting, every time. If something is already going out in that slot, hold yours for the next one. On the drop page in §09 that is the hold it for a date option, and on a phone it is about fifteen seconds.
  • If the photograph in your hand is better than what is scheduled, say so and swap it rather than running both. A great omelette on a Tuesday beats a planned post about the same meal, and two posts an hour apart are worth less than either alone. This one needs a person to decide, which is why it is a convention and not a rule.

08

Stage one, this week, with no new software

This is the part to print and put where the staff can see it. It is five steps and every one of them uses something the team already has on their phones.

  1. One album instead of an inbox

    Make one shared phone album called Fenimore's Posts. A shared iPhone album or a shared Google Photos album both work and both are already on everyone's phone. Add everyone who takes pictures. From that point, nobody emails a photograph again.

  2. Send three things with the picture, not one

    In the album comment: one line in your own words about what it is, where it should send people if anywhere, and anything worth tagging. Short rib grilled cheese, back on the lunch menu today. Link to the lunch menu. Nobody to tag.

    Eight seconds, and it is what makes the finished post sound like Fenimore's rather than like a template. Whoever posts it then handles the platform difference in §05, which is not the photographer's problem to solve.

  3. One name per stretch of the week

    Somebody is the poster for weekday daytime. Somebody is the poster from Friday afternoon through Sunday night, which is when the restaurant is busiest and the pictures are best. Two names, written on the wall. Who those two people are is the room's call, not ours.

  4. Check the planner, then post at the right hour

    Open the planner first and see what is already scheduled for today, per §07. Then post into a gap: morning, mid-afternoon, early evening, per the grid above. One post landing at the hour someone is deciding where to eat is worth more than four posted at midnight, and two landing an hour apart cancel each other out.

  5. Say posted out loud

    One message in the group text when it goes up. That single word is what ends the I thought you were doing it problem, and it costs nothing to add.

The honest risk with stage one is that shared albums die quietly. Everyone joins, four people post for a week, and then it goes still with nobody deciding to stop. That is worth naming in advance because it is also the most useful result the two weeks can produce. If the album is empty by the second Friday, the friction is somewhere this document has not found, and building anything on top of it would be building on sand. Two weeks is a cheap way to find that out.

09

Stage two: a drop point built for a phone at the pass

A shared album is a good start and it has a ceiling. It holds no schedule, it carries no categories, and it tells nobody that something new has arrived. The next step is a page built for one job.

Staff page · fenimorestable.com, code protected, not linked from the site

Photo · taken just now or picked from the roll

What is it · breakfast / lunch special / drink / dinner / the room / game day / event

One line in your words · the sentence from step two above

Send people where · breakfast menu / full menu / drinks / order / reserve / nowhere

Anyone to tag · left blank most of the time, and the location tag is added automatically

When · today, or hold it for a date

Shown at the top of the page · what is already scheduled today, so nobody has to remember to go and look

What happens behind it: the picture lands in one queue with a timestamp and a category, the person on duty gets a notification that something arrived, three caption options get drafted from that one line in the restaurant's voice, and the post is slotted against the right hour rather than whenever someone next opens the app. It also writes the two endings from §05, so the Facebook version carries the real URL and the Instagram version points at the bio, without anyone having to hold that rule in their head at seven in the morning. A person picks a caption, edits it, and sends it. Nothing posts unread.

From the floor it is about twenty seconds standing at the pass. Nobody installs an app, nobody has a password to remember, and nobody has to wonder afterwards where the picture went.

Building this sits inside the current arrangement. It is not a new line item and there is no budget conversation attached to it. It is also the same shape for the Clubhouse, so if it works at Fenimore's it carries next door without being rebuilt.

11

Stage three, once stage one is a habit: video

Reels and short video came up on the call, along with the observation that there are people around, some of them young enough to have grown up doing it, who would happily shoot them. Both of those are right, and video is where the audience actually is in a town where social carries this much weight.

It is stage three for one reason. A team that does not yet have a working path for a still photograph will not sustain a video schedule, and a video programme that stalls after ten days does more harm than never starting. When the album is a habit and the daily post is landing at the right hour, the useful questions become frequency, who edits, and whether it is a paid arrangement.

12

What we need answered before building anything

  1. Who are the two names? One for weekday daytime, one for Friday afternoon through Sunday. This is the only item that blocks everything else, and it is a decision for the room rather than a question we can research.
  2. Who currently has full control of the Facebook Page, and will they grant task-level access to those two names? This is the second blocker and it is the practical form of a question asked on the call that never landed on an answer. Nobody is being asked to give up anything: the grant takes a few minutes, it can be withdrawn in one, and it involves no password changing hands. See §04.
  3. Is the Instagram account a professional account and linked to the Facebook Page? If it is not, that has to happen before anyone can be added or anything scheduled, and it is invisible to us from outside.
  4. What does the Instagram bio link point at today? Someone needs to look on a phone. If Instagram posts are going to say link in bio, that link is doing real work, and the bio currently makes no mention of breakfast.
  5. Any objection to a staff-only upload page on fenimorestable.com? It would be code protected and linked from nowhere on the public site.
  6. Is anyone already scheduling in Meta Business Suite, how far ahead is the schedule built, and who would build the football and holiday backbone in §07? If posts are already planned a month out, stage two should plug into that rather than sit beside it, and everyone who posts needs to be able to see it from day one. The backbone itself is a sitting or two of work for a whole season, and it is worth doing before the football weekends and the holiday stretch rather than during them.

13

What this document does not do

It moves no account and asks nobody to hand over a password. Whoever administers @fenimorestable today still administers it after every stage described here. The access in §04 is granted by them, sits below their own, and can be withdrawn by them in about a minute.

It does not propose taking work away from anyone. The division in §07 hands the planned calendar to the people already doing that kind of work, on a weekday, with lead time, and hands the same-day photograph to the people holding the camera. That is a description of where each job is easiest to do well, not a judgment about anyone's hours. How those hours are allocated stays a conversation for the people in it.

It does not touch the website, the private events pages or the menu work. Those are separate and have their own drafts.

It assumes nothing about what has or has not been posted so far. Everything here is about the path forward.

If any of this is wrong, or if there is already a path that works and we have missed it, say so and we will rebuild this around what is actually there.
