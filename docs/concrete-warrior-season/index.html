Why topsoil leads even though concrete is close on net

Reclamation topsoil moves in jobs of eight to ten thousand yards, where haul distance is most of the landed cost, and nobody searching for topsoil types the word concrete. Which line earns more in a given week changes neither of those things. Knife River operates ready mix in New Town, so proximity there is table stakes rather than an advantage, and the concrete page has to earn its position on findability, published specifications and real answers instead.

Both of the volumes carrying the back half of the season are changes to the plan rather than results, and the 4 September answers split them cleanly. On topsoil the capacity is proven and the demand is not: week 2 delivered 11,800 yards against a 10,000 plan, so the pit can make the number, while Jeff puts the sales position as unknown. On concrete neither half is proven: the plant went live on 24 August and its 20 yards went to onsite needs. Together the two lines carry $620,000 of the $620,000 weekly revenue from week 9 onward, which is to say all of it. Capacity is the easier of the two problems to fix, and it is the one already solved.

07

The case for building findability now

Why the digital work should not wait for spring

Production stops 15 November and does not restart until the middle of March. That is roughly 17 weeks of no revenue in North Dakota, and the only stretch of the year when the website and the Google listing are the entire business.

  • Buyers plan in February, when nothing is running

    A contractor bidding a spring pour, a county setting its 2027 road program, an operator scheduling reclamation. Whatever is findable in February decides who gets the call in April.

  • A listing built in March is brand new in March

    Reviews, photographs and local ranking build with the age and activity of a profile. Built now it spends the winter maturing. Built in March, the 2027 season opens with about six weeks of being invisible.

  • Verification and photography are easier during the season

    Google’s video check needs somebody on site, and the yard photographs well while equipment is running and the ground is not frozen. Neither is true of a yard that is just reopening.

  • 93% of the season plan needs buyers who have not been named yet

    Jeff’s 4 September note makes the topsoil and concrete volumes production figures rather than orders. Whatever closes that gap is demand work, and the website and the listing are the only demand work on this dashboard.

  • The recurring cost is three and a half thousandths of one percent

    Email is about $168 a year, the domain renewal is $11.25, and the listing is free, against a season modelled at $5.05 million net. It is also the only line on this dashboard that keeps working after 15 November.

What the winter is actually for

The equipment goes south and the North Dakota crew stands down. The work that can only be done when nobody is busy is exactly this: finishing the website, letting the listing age, publishing the material specifications and gradations, and answering the questions buyers ask before they call. None of it competes with production, because there is no production.

The 2027 season runs from roughly mid-March to 15 November, about 35 weeks. Opening that season already findable is worth more than anything that can be done to the site once it has started. Billing is settled and sits on the Buffalo side from day one, which avoids a handover when the domain transfers.

What this is built on

Read before circulating any figure here

Every figure on this page comes from Richard and Jeff’s workbook or is derived from it. The model has not changed since 25 August. Jeff answered four standing questions on 4 September, and they change how the model should be read rather than any number in it. Four things are worth knowing before any of it is repeated outside this group.

The revenue lines are production multiplied by price

This is the 4 September answer and it is the most important thing on the page. 10,000 topsoil yards a week is what the pit produces; the sales position is unknown. The concrete produced so far went to onsite needs and was not sold. The workbook takes yards, multiplies by $32 and $300, and books the result as revenue, which is a reasonable way to plan a yard and is not a forecast of receipts. Demo, at $525,000 across eight weeks, is the only line on the page that is billed work, and it is 7% of the season.

Every dollar on this page is plan

The daily log records topsoil yards, rubble truck counts and, since 28 August, concrete yards. Nothing on the expense side is tracked, so every dollar on this page is plan. The empty crushing and demo columns are now explained: Jeff fills them when that work is done, and there has been none recently, so they read as a schedule signal rather than a reporting gap. What is still missing is an actual cost for the weeks that have run — wages, diesel, rentals and finance payments accrue whether or not crushing does.

Two columns look like measurements and are not. Rubble tonnage is truck count multiplied by an assumed 15 tons, and the sand column is load count multiplied by an assumed 28. Nothing is weighed. They carry no more information than the counts they are derived from.

69.2% net is a contribution margin, not a full profit and loss

Aggregate producers typically run 20 to 35% EBITDA. Going through the expense side line by line, the following are absent:

  • No hauling cost on topsoil at all. Concrete carries a $130 delivery line and topsoil carries none. If topsoil is sold at the pit and the customer hauls it, that is correct and the margin stands. Nobody has confirmed which.
  • No insurance, bonding or workers compensation beyond flat wage lines
  • No TERO fees, which apply on Fort Berthold
  • No taxes, and no royalty beyond $4,000 a month of land rent
  • General and administrative expense is a single $5,000 a week line
  • One manpower line for topsoil, four men at $6,000 a week. Confirmed correct by Jeff on 4 September: two trommels run on a crew of four in total, so the flat line is the real one and no second crew sits outside it

That is a normal thing for an operator to build and a useful thing to run a yard against. It is not the number to put in front of a lender or a partner without saying what it excludes.

Nobody has tested the $300 concrete price

The workbook states $32 a yard for topsoil and $300 a yard for concrete, and both reconcile exactly against the weekly revenue lines. That confirms the rate the model uses, not that the market pays it. Knife River runs ready mix and aggregate in New Town itself, so $300 is an assumption worth testing rather than a given.
