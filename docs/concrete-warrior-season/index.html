Concrete doubled this week, and the two lines have converged

This version of the model runs concrete at 1,000 yards a week from week 5, twice what last week’s carried. Season revenue now splits 48% topsoil and 45% concrete, where seven days ago it was 60 and 32. The two lines have also converged on net: a steady topsoil week returns $208,814 against concrete’s $166,826, a gap of about a quarter where it used to be three to one.

What this does not do is decide which line the site leads with. That call was made on the 23 and 24 August operating calls, and it rests on freight and on how buyers search rather than on margin. Reclamation topsoil moves in jobs of eight to ten thousand yards, where haul distance is most of the landed cost, and nobody searching for topsoil types the word concrete. Concrete earning more per week changes neither of those things. The build spec carries the current answer: topsoil leads, and concrete keeps its own page and its own argument, since ready-mix cannot be trucked in from a distance at all.

One caution before this gets repeated anywhere. The 1,000 yard figure is a change to the plan, not a result. Nothing in the workbook says whether it is sold volume or plant capacity, and $300,000 of weekly revenue rests on it. See section 08.

07

The case for building findability now

Why the digital work should not wait for spring

Production stops 15 November and does not restart until the middle of March. That is roughly 17 weeks of no revenue in North Dakota. It is also the only stretch of the year when the website and the Google listing are the entire business.

Search does not take the winter off

A contractor bidding a spring pour, a county planning its 2027 road program, an oilfield operator scheduling reclamation, all of them do that work in January and February. They search while nothing is running and nobody is answering the phone at the yard. Whatever is findable in February decides who gets the call in April.

A profile built in March is brand new in March

Google listings do not switch on at full strength. Reviews accumulate, photographs accumulate, questions get asked and answered, and local ranking builds with the age and activity of the profile. A listing created in March has none of that during the exact weeks it would be worth the most.

Built now, it spends the winter quietly maturing and arrives at the March restart already established. Built in March, the 2027 season opens with roughly six weeks of being invisible.

Build now

Listing verified during the season while people are on site to answer Google's video check. Photographs taken while equipment is running and the ground is not frozen. Four months of quiet maturing. Findable when the February planning happens.

Build in March

Verification needs somebody at a yard that is just reopening. No summer photographs available. Profile is brand new during the season's first six weeks. February searches find nothing.

The cost sits next to the wrong number to argue about

The business email account runs about $168 a year and the domain renewal is $11.25. The Google listing itself is free. Measured against a season modelled at $3.67 million net, the recurring digital cost is roughly five thousandths of one percent.

It is also the only line on this dashboard that keeps working after 15 November.

What the winter is actually for

The equipment goes south, and the North Dakota crew stands down. The work that can only be done when nobody is busy is exactly this: finishing the website, letting the listing age, publishing the material specifications and gradations, and answering the questions buyers ask before they call. None of it competes with production, because there is no production.

The 2027 season runs from roughly mid-March to 15 November, about 35 weeks. Opening that season already findable is worth more than anything that can be done to the site once it has started.

What this is built on

Read before circulating any figure here

What changed since Friday

The 23 August workbook ran concrete at 500 yards a week from week 5. This one runs 1,000, and the cost lines move with it: portland doubles to $45,750 a week, concrete delivery to $39,000, diesel and miscellaneous to $5,000 each. Topsoil, demo, wages, rentals and finance payments are all untouched. That one change adds $1,350,000 of revenue and $898,650 of net to the thirteen-week season, and it is the entire difference between last week’s figures and these.

It is now the largest single assumption on this page. Doubling output on paper is free, and the workbook gives no basis for it, so it is worth asking Richard directly whether 1,000 yards a week is volume already spoken for or the ceiling of what the plant can pour.

A second change is bookkeeping rather than money. The line labelled “Material Cost, $1.50 per yard” was in fact charging $2.00 a yard. It has been split into $1.50 of material and $0.50 of round-up, which totals the same $2.00. No figure on this page moves. The sheet now says what it was already doing.

The daily log did not change. It still holds the five days of week 1, which is correct, since week 2 begins today.

The margin needs a conversation

The model shows 67.3% net. Aggregate producers typically run 20 to 35% EBITDA. Going through the expense side line by line, the following are absent:

  • No hauling cost on topsoil at all. Concrete carries a $130 delivery line; topsoil carries none. If topsoil is sold at the pit and the customer hauls it, that is correct and the margin stands. Nobody has confirmed which.
  • No insurance, bonding or workers compensation beyond flat wage lines
  • No TERO fees, which apply on Fort Berthold
  • No taxes, and no royalty beyond $4,000 a month of land rent
  • General and administrative expense is a single $5,000 a week line

This reads as a contribution margin model rather than a full profit and loss. That is a normal thing for an operator to build and a useful thing to run a yard against. It is not the number to put in front of a lender or a partner without saying what it excludes.

What the dashboard cannot yet show

The daily log captures topsoil yards and rubble trucks. Those are real and they are enough to track volume against plan. It does not capture concrete yards produced, tons crushed, demo dollars billed, or actual costs, which is why every dollar figure above is plan rather than plan against actual. Adding four fields to the sheet closes that gap and costs whoever fills it in about a minute a day.

Prices, derived rather than stated

Unit prices appear nowhere in the workbook. Working backward from three separate weeks gives $32 a yard for topsoil and $300 a yard for concrete, and both reconcile exactly across all thirteen weeks. The concrete figure is well above a typical ready-mix price, which is plausible given the nearest plant is 60 miles away, but it is an assumption worth confirming rather than a quoted rate.

The season stop

The workbook runs fifteen weeks to 29 November. Cutting at the 15 November shutdown removes two weeks, $1,112,000 of revenue and $763,780 of net. Everything on this page is the thirteen-week version. Weather moves this date in both directions.
