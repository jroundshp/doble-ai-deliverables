Closest-job system: the full plan

Larry's day with it, what VPC gets out of it, how Corey owns it so it survives whether or not Larry is here, why Grok Bot is the front door and not the filing cabinet, and every step between today and a tool people use on Monday morning.

Metering manholes and flumes first. Shelters come later, once there are enough of them to learn from. Companion document: the submittal library concept, which is where the twenty packages and the ranking output are written up. The website work order is a separate job and nothing here waits on it.

What we are after

A job that comes in should land next to the jobs like it, with the reusable parts already identified and the parts that have to change already flagged.

  • FindableThe closest past job comes back because it matches, not because somebody remembered it.
  • Carried forwardWhat repeats arrives already assembled. What changes gets asked about instead of inherited quietly.
  • Owned by the companyThe history lives on VPC's storage under VPC's accounts, so it does not walk out with any one person.

None of this replaces the engineering. It removes the retrieval and the retyping in front of it.

01

What the system is

One thing: a closest-job index that runs on every RFQ before anybody opens a drawing. Describe the job coming in, get back the past jobs that are nearest to it, ranked, with a line on each saying what is different.

The loop, start to finish:

1RFQ arrivesSales enters it and Salesforce issues the number, the same as today.
2Drop the order inThe sales order goes into the tool. Nothing is retyped and nothing on the drive moves.
3Indexed history answersEvery past job is already fingerprinted, so the match happens against the whole record rather than against the folder somebody thinks to open.
4Ranked neighboursFour or five past jobs, closest first, each with its carry list and its change list.
5Start from the packageOpen the winner, work from it, and answer the flagged fields rather than hunting for them.

It does not generate CAD. It does not decide what a job needs, which is engineering judgement and stays with Larry. And it is not the PDF library handed to a chatbot, which gives confident answers with nothing underneath them.

It already runs. Treat job 69492 as the RFQ that just came in. The index puts 65067 on top at 76 percent, then lists three fields that carry over unchanged and three that have to change. That output is real, off the existing filenames, with nothing on VPC's drive renamed or moved.

02

Why it is worth building

Five to ten submittals a week come through. A metering manhole runs three to five hours, and Larry's estimate is that sixty to seventy percent of one is already sitting somewhere in a job that was finished months ago.

That figure is a manhole figure. It was given about metering manholes, because that is what the question on the call was about, and it should not be stretched across the whole week until somebody has timed a shelter.

The retrieval itself is the part that fails quietly. Searching the submittal subdirectory by type works when the spelling holds, and the spelling does not always hold. Search the drive for ultra bkt and you get thirteen of the seventeen jobs that have one; the other four are typed ulta bkt or ultra kkt and simply do not come back. Nothing on the screen tells you four are missing. An index that normalises as it reads finds all seventeen, and nobody has to change how they name anything.

Underneath that sits a bigger gap. The order lives in Salesforce and the drawing lives on the server, and the only thing that joins the two is Larry.

The limit is worth naming now rather than later. An index built on finished packages answers what VPC has built before. It cannot answer what this job ought to be until the sales orders are in it, which is why they are the first ask in section 09.

03

What VPC gets

SpeedThe hunting stretch of a submittal shortens. On manholes that is the front end of a three to five hour job.
CapacityMore submittals move through the same headcount, which matters most in the weeks that run at ten rather than five.
ConsistencyTwo people starting from the same closest job produce packages that look like each other, which is the point of the folder scheme Dave Riggs already put in place.
Company knowledgeYears of configuration history stop being a naming habit and become something the company can query.
ContinuityIf Larry takes a month off or retires, the record of what VPC has built is still readable by whoever sits down next.
Quote speedThe same index, asked what a job like this quoted at and what it turned out to be. That is the path to answering a customer faster, and it needs the sales orders.

This runs on its own track. It does not touch the website, it needs nothing from Amanda, and neither project can hold the other up.

04

What Larry gets

The hour that goes into finding the right past job and retyping values out of it comes back. That is the whole offer, and it is deliberately narrow.

  • Carry and change come back separated. The fields that repeat arrive filled. The ones that have to be re-established for this job, elevations and pipe stubs and project and customer, come up as questions rather than travelling across quietly from the job before.
  • It runs on his own convention. The vocabulary in the index is the one already in the filenames. Nobody is asking him to learn a new one or to rename a single file.
  • His cheat sheet is the head start rather than something this competes with. He has kept a spreadsheet of schematics and components for years, which means a hand-built index of this work already exists. We would rather learn from it than duplicate it.
  • He confirms the rankings. When the tool puts a past job on top, Larry says whether that is the one he would have opened. Where he disagrees, the scoring changes, not his answer.
  • Shelters stay out until he says otherwise. They are the larger share of his week and a different animal, and nothing about this approach is being assumed to carry.

The tool takes a position on which past job is closest. Larry is the one who decides whether it is right, and that is true after it goes live, not only during the test.

05

Ownership, so it survives a departure

This is the part that decides whether VPC has an asset or a dependency. It is worth settling before anything is built, because retrofitting ownership onto a tool somebody has been using for six months is a much worse conversation.

OwnerCorey and VPC. Not Doble AI, and not Larry.
Larry's roleThe principal user and the subject-matter authority. He decides whether a ranking is right. He is not the account holder.
AccountsCompany credentials and company hosting from day one, on a VPC domain or a VPC-owned workspace. No personal logins anywhere in the chain.
The corpusThe job records live on VPC storage, the L and M drives or wherever Corey lands after the Lockport move. The index is derived from them and can be rebuilt from them.
RunbookA short written document: where the data sits, how to re-index, who to call, how to hand it to a different vendor. Written as we build, not after.
Second userSomebody besides Larry uses it from the first week. Conrad or Taylor is the obvious candidate given they are already on the same submittal format.
Re-indexA documented, repeatable job that anyone can run when new work lands, not a favour to ask us for.
Corey's viewCounts he can read without asking anyone: jobs indexed, searches run, which product families are actually using it.

The test for whether this is owned. If Larry and Doble AI both disappeared tomorrow, could VPC point a new person or a new vendor at the data and the runbook and carry on? If the answer is anything other than yes, the setup is wrong and it is cheap to fix now.

06

Where Grok Bot fits

Grok Bot is a good front door. Larry types a job description the way he would say it out loud, and gets the ranked list back without learning a query language. That is a real benefit and it is why it is in the plan.

It is not where the history lives.

Interfaceyes   Ask it in plain language, read the answer, follow it into the package.
Vaultno   The job records and the index sit on VPC storage. The agent reads them; it does not hold them.

The reason is continuity again. Anything that exists only inside an agent's memory is gone when the account lapses, the vendor changes or the model is swapped, and there is no way to audit what it thinks it knows. Keeping the corpus on VPC's side means the front door can be replaced without losing a single job record, and a second interface can be added later without rebuilding anything underneath.

07

From here to production

Eight phases. Each one is useful on its own, and each gets built only if the one before it earned it.

0AlignAgree the scope, the ownership model in section 05 and what a good result looks like. Corey confirms ownership and access. One short conversation.
1Corpus and indexPoint at the manhole and flume history on the server, fingerprint every job, and reconcile against Larry's cheat sheet. The output is a count of jobs indexed and a list of anything that would not parse.
2Five-job testFive live jobs through the index. Does it put the right past job on top, and is the difference list the one Larry would have written? About an hour of his time, and it is the phase that decides whether the rest happens.
3Daily toolIf the test holds, make it usable every day: company hosting, company accounts, the Grok Bot front door, a second user, and the runbook written as we go.
4Start from itOnly if phase 3 is genuinely in use. The closest job comes back as a starting package with the job-specific fields pulled out and asked about. Needs Larry's list of which fields are always job-specific.
5Quote historyAdd the sales orders so the index can answer what a job like this was quoted at, and what it ended up being. This is where turnaround on quoting improves rather than on drafting.
6SheltersExtend to shelters once there are around ten with their packages. Sixty percent of the week, a different structure, and worth doing properly rather than early.
7ContinuityHand over: runbook finished, re-index proved by somebody at VPC running it, access confirmed, Corey's counts in place.

Phases 0 through 2 are the near-term commitment. Everything after phase 2 is a decision Corey makes with the test result in front of him.

08

What we are not doing yet

  • No CPQ purchase. Configure-price-quote software is a real category and it may be the right answer one day. Buying one before the history is indexed means paying for a system nobody can populate.
  • No CAD generation. It was called dreamland in the room and that is the right label for now. SolidWorks stays where it is.
  • No chatbot dump. Loading the PDF library into a general chatbot and asking it questions produces answers that sound right and cannot be checked. The index gives a job number to open.
  • No dependency on the website. The work order for vpcfiberglass.com is a separate project on a separate track, and neither one is a prerequisite for the other.

Each of these can be revisited. None of them should be started on the strength of a first result.
