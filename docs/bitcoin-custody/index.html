A decision tree

Exposure, or ownership

Every question about how to hold bitcoin comes down to one fork, asked three times: do you want the thing, or a claim on the thing? Here is the map, and what waits at the end of each branch.

The whole map

Three forks, four destinations

The first fork is the real one. If you only want the price to go up, a spot ETF is a perfectly rational instrument and the rest of this document is optional reading. If you want the asset itself, everything after that is a question about who can take it from you and how easily you can take it back.

These paths are not exclusive. A common arrangement is an ETF inside the retirement account, where the tax wrapper is worth more than the sovereignty, and coins in cold storage outside it, where the reverse is true.

The first fork, in plain terms

The ETF is a claim. The coin is the thing.

The gold analogy holds almost exactly. An ETF share is a claim on metal in someone else's vault, priced continuously and settled in dollars. It is convenient, liquid, and it sits inside a retirement account. It is also a promise that a chain of institutions will keep behaving.

Two mechanical details are worth knowing. The sponsor fee is paid in bitcoin, so the amount of BTC backing each share declines a little every year even when the price does nothing. And ordinary shareholders cannot redeem shares for coins. The exit is dollars, at market hours, through a broker.

Self-custody buys you the opposite trade: no fee drag, no market hours, no counterparty, and no one to call when you make a mistake. You become the vault, the backup department, and the estate plan.

Inside the self-custody branch

Two secrets, two places, one wallet

This is the piece people find counterintuitive, so it is worth drawing. The hardware device is not where your bitcoin lives. The device is a replaceable tool that signs. What actually controls the money is a pair of secrets, and the design goal is that finding either one alone gets an attacker nowhere.

The fourth case, and the one that actually happens

You are the only living person who knows that both halves exist, what they are for, and where they sit. Then you are gone, and your family inherits a metal plate they cannot read and a password they never knew to look for. Separation defeats a burglar and an heir with equal efficiency.

Threat modeling

People defend against the wrong things

Almost nobody loses coins the way they imagine losing coins. The failures that actually empty wallets are quiet, procedural, and boring, and they cluster in the corner of this chart that gets the least attention.

Doing it right

The order matters more than the gear

Every step below exists because skipping it is a documented way to lose money. The sequence is deliberate: everything that could go wrong should go wrong while the wallet is empty. Note that both secrets come off the same pair of dice, which is the point. Randomness you produced in a room by yourself is the one input in this whole process that no manufacturer, firmware, or supply chain can quietly compromise.

  1. Buy the device from the manufacturerDirect, never a marketplace listing. A tampered device is the one attack that defeats every other precaution. Check the firmware signature on first boot.
  2. Roll the seed yourself, with dice, offlineThis is the recommendation, not the advanced option. A hardware wallet's random number generator is the one component you cannot audit, and every word of your seed comes out of it. Dice remove it from the trust equation. Ninety-nine rolls of a fair six-sided die yields 256 bits, which is exactly what a 24-word seed encodes. Enter each roll into the device and let it derive the words and the checksum, rather than converting by hand. If the device offers to mix your dice entropy with its own, take it: the result is safe as long as either source was.
  3. Write the seed to steel, not paperEngraved or stamped, rated for house-fire temperatures. Paper is a five-year medium in a drawer and a zero-year medium in a flood.
  4. Roll the passphrase with dice tooUse the same method and the same dice. Five rolls select one word from a standard diceware list; six words gives you roughly 77 bits, far more than any phrase you would invent, and it is words rather than symbols so you can actually memorize it. Understand what you are making: this is a second wallet, not a second password. There is no checksum and no error message, so one wrong character silently opens a different empty wallet that looks entirely legitimate. Record capitalization and spacing exactly as entered. If you are not confident you can reproduce it in ten years, a plain seed you can genuinely recover beats a clever one you cannot.
  5. Separate the two secretsFar enough apart that one burglary, one fire, or one flood cannot take both. Close enough that you can still assemble them yourself in an afternoon.
  6. Wipe the device and restore from the backupBefore a single satoshi goes in. This is the step that gets skipped and the reason people discover a transcription error years too late. If the restored wallet shows the same first receive address, your backup is real.
  7. Send a small test amount, then the restVerify the receiving address on the device screen itself, not on the computer. Address-swapping malware edits what your monitor shows you and cannot edit what the device shows you.
  8. Write the instructions for whoever comes after youSealed, with the lawyer or in the safe: what the metal plate is, which device, that a passphrase exists and where it lives, and who to call. Not the passphrase itself.
  9. Say nothingThe most effective physical security measure is that nobody knows there is anything to take.
Choosing between them

This is not a ladder

It is tempting to read these three as tiers, with multisig at the top for serious money. That framing is wrong, and following it has cost people coins. Multisig does not add security on top of a good single-signature setup. It trades one category of risk for another: it removes any single key, safe, or fire as a fatal event, and in exchange it adds coordination, a descriptor you must back up everywhere, more software to keep working, and an inheritance problem that is genuinely harder to write down.

A dice-generated seed, a passphrase you can reproduce, steel backups, real geographic separation, and clear instructions for your heirs is a complete strategy. Nothing sits above it that you are obligated to graduate to. Choose by which failure you are actually trying to prevent, and by who has to operate this when you are having a bad day years from now.

SetupChoose it whenWhat it costs you
One hardware wallet, seed on steel, no passphrase You want the fewest moving parts in existence, one physical location is genuinely secure, and whoever inherits this may not be technical. It is also the right place to learn, before real money is involved. Anyone who finds the plate has the coins. Your entire defense is physical.
Single signature plus a passphrase, in two locations You want a discovered backup to be worth nothing on its own, one person operates it, and you are confident you can reproduce the passphrase exactly in ten years. A duress wallet comes free with the design. The passphrase has no checksum and no reset. Forgetting it is indistinguishable from never having had the coins.
2-of-3 multisig No single location, event, or person should be able to end this: a house fire, a coerced moment, or a co-owner. Often the right answer when control is genuinely shared, as with partners, a business, or a trust. More software, more parts, and a descriptor that becomes its own single point of failure the moment you neglect it. Harder to hand to an heir.

Sovereignty is not a feature you buy once. It is an ongoing job you have agreed to do yourself, and the whole argument for doing it is that no one else can be fired from it, go bankrupt during it, or decide one morning that you no longer qualify.
