Water Rights in the Vail Valley: What a Call on the Eagle River Changes for Buyers

September 11, 2026 · By John Tyler

On August 14 the Colorado Division of Water Resources placed a call on the Eagle River. For most of the time I have been selling property in this valley, that sentence would have meant nothing to a buyer. It means something now.

I am not a water attorney and I am not an engineer. Where any particular property stands is their work rather than mine, and for anything with a ditch or a well attached it is worth paying one of them early rather than late. What I can lay out is what changed this summer, what it tends to mean depending on what you are buying, and which questions are worth asking while walking away is still an option.

The short version is that water has moved from the background of a purchase to the front of it. It arrives differently depending on whether you are looking at a ranch, a lot with a well, or a residence whose association pays the water bill. The last of those surprises people the most, because none of it is visible at a showing.

What a call on the river actually means

Colorado allocates water by priority rather than by ownership of the land it runs past. The doctrine is called prior appropriation and it gets summarized as first in time, first in right. Every decreed water right carries a priority date, and in a year when there is not enough water for everyone, the oldest rights are satisfied first and the newest go without.

A call is what happens when a senior right stops getting its water and the state enforces that order. On August 14 the Eagle dropped below a minimum streamflow decreed on October 7, 1981, which requires 85 cubic feet per second between Gore Creek and Lake Creek from May 1 through September 30, and 35 cfs through the winter. The river was running around 40. Junior users in that reach now have to stop diverting or replace what they take with water from somewhere else.

Two things about that are worth holding onto. What determines whether a right still produces water is the year it was decreed, not the volume it is for or what somebody paid for it. And the rights that kept diverting through August on this stretch include golf courses sitting on historic agricultural rights that predate most of what is residential in the valley. A 1970s subdivision well and an 1890s ditch are in entirely different positions, and nothing in a listing tells you which one you are standing on. It is the same river people drive across the valley to fish, and this year it has been the subject of a legal proceeding rather than a fishing report.

Buying a ranch, and what the deed does not tell you

Water rights in Colorado are real property. They can be sold with land and they can be sold away from it. A ranch that irrigated eighty acres in 1970 may or may not still hold the right that made that possible, and a listing photograph of a green hay meadow is not evidence either way.

A deed conveying the property “together with all water rights appurtenant thereto” reads reassuringly and says almost nothing. What says something is the list: case numbers, decreed amounts, priority dates, the structure each right is tied to, and whether a right has been through water court to change its use from the one it was decreed for. Division 5 water court, which covers this basin, sits in Glenwood Springs and its files are public. Ditch shares are a separate matter again. They are corporate stock, transferred on the ditch company books rather than through the deed, and a closing that overlooks them leaves the water behind with the seller.

Then there is use. Colorado measures a right by what it has historically consumed rather than by the number printed on the decree, so a right that has been sitting idle is worth less than its paper amount and can be worth nothing. The division engineers publish a decennial abandonment list of rights they believe have been abandoned through non-use. The last one came out in 2020 and the next is due in 2030, which means the decade currently being observed is this one. A property whose ditch has not run in eight years deserves harder questions than a property whose ditch ran last month. For a buyer treating the purchase as an investment, this is also the kind of thing worth settling before a 1031 exchange clock starts running, because the identification window does not pause while a title company chases a water decree.

One more thing about ranches in particular. If the plan is to stop irrigating the hay meadow and put in lawn, horses, and a pond, that is not a landscaping decision. Changing what a decreed right is used for is a water court proceeding, it gets measured against what the right has historically consumed rather than what the decree says, and it takes time and money that almost nobody budgets for at closing. A buyer who intends to keep farming it the way the seller farmed it has a far simpler path than a buyer who intends to change it.

The well permit question, which catches people

Below the ranch scale, the thing that catches buyers is the permit, because the phrase “it has a well” covers documents that do very different things.

Most wells drilled on parcels under 35 acres since May of 1972 are permitted for household use only. That means indoor use in one single-family home at up to 15 gallons a minute. No lawn irrigation and no livestock. Buyers picturing a garden, a couple of horses, and a hose running in July are picturing a domestic permit, which generally requires 35 acres or more and allows up to three single-family homes, an acre of lawn and garden, and domestic animals.

Smaller parcels inside a platted subdivision sit in a third position, where even a household-use permit may be unavailable and the lot depends instead on being inside a court-decreed plan for augmentation. Augmentation is the mechanism that allows water to be used out of priority by replacing the depletion with water from another source, usually storage. It is the reason a subdivision keeps its taps on during a call that would otherwise shut off a junior well. It is also a standing annual obligation with a cost attached, and on a subdivision lot the party carrying that cost is usually the association.

Where this lands in an HOA budget

Most buyers here are not buying a well at all. They are buying a residence served by the Eagle River Water and Sanitation District or by the Upper Eagle Regional Water Authority, and the water arrives the way it always has. What changed is the cost and who absorbs it.

The district declared a water shortage on April 9 and moved to Stage III restrictions: outdoor watering two days a week assigned by street address, and only between midnight and 8 a.m. or 8 p.m. and midnight. Rates that took effect December 1 raised the combined water and wastewater base for a typical residential customer by about 7 percent, with more than half of that going to capital work on what is now the sixth largest water system in the state. The increases are deliberately uneven. The top two usage tiers went up 10 and 20 percent, and Siri Roman, the district general manager, has been direct about the intent: customers who use water responsibly would see minimal increases, while there would be significant cost impacts for customers who use water excessively.

Above the rates sit surcharges and fines. Tier four use carries a 15 percent surcharge. Tier five carries a flat 400 dollar fine plus escalating per-gallon charges. Watering off schedule draws a 500 dollar fine, and a fourth violation inside twelve months can mean a flow restricting device installed on the meter at the customer's expense. Dick Cleveland, who chairs the district board, put the reasoning plainly: “Fines are one of our only tools to influence customer behavior.” About 14 percent of residential accounts sit in tiers four and five, and they account for roughly 45 percent of residential water use.

Now apply that to an association. A condominium building or a subdivision with common-area landscaping is frequently one account on one master meter. Entry landscaping, a lawn along the drive, and a green stretch in front of the clubhouse can put the association itself into the upper tiers, and the surcharge that follows is not billed to whichever owner left a zone running. It is billed to the association, and it comes back to every owner through dues or, after a bad enough season, through a special assessment. This is the part of a drought that does not look like a drought on a statement. It looks like a line item.

Two changes in state law push the same direction. Since January 1 of this year, associations with more than twelve dwelling units cannot install nonfunctional turf, artificial turf, or invasive species on their common elements. That governs new installation and redevelopment rather than forcing anyone to tear out an existing lawn, but an association replacing an irrigation system or rebuilding a common area is now doing it under a rule that did not exist in 2024. The Town of Vail has been working toward the same end from the other direction, with a land use code that has allowed roughly 3,000 to 6,500 square feet of irrigated turf on a new home and a proposal on the table to cut that to 500. Given how different the communities in this valley are from one another, the same change reaches a Vail condominium and an Edwards subdivision very differently.

Some of it arrives on the tax bill rather than the dues statement. Last November voters approved Ballot Issue 6A by 70 percent to 30 percent, authorizing a 93 million dollar general obligation bond for nutrient upgrades the state requires at the regional wastewater plant in Edwards by 2029. The district estimated the cost at roughly 112 dollars a year on a home valued at a million dollars. That is not a water rate and not an HOA fee. It is a third place the same problem shows up.

The obvious counterweight is worth saying out loud. For most purchases in this valley, none of this changes anything at all. The tap works, the association pays the bill, the buyer never learns what a priority date is, and that has held through every dry year I have worked here. The reason to ask anyway is that the cases where it does matter are not signposted in advance, and the questions cost an afternoon.

The questions worth asking during the inspection period

None of this asks a buyer to become an expert. It asks for a short list of questions early enough that the answers can still change the decision.

  • Is this community on district water, on a shared central well, or on individual wells
  • If there is a well, what is the permit number and what does that permit actually allow
  • Is the property inside a decreed plan for augmentation, and what does the association pay into it annually
  • Is the association master metered or individually metered, and which usage tier did it finish in last August
  • Does the reserve study cover the irrigation system, or does it stop at roofs, asphalt, and paint
  • Has the association been surcharged or fined in the last two seasons, and does it appear in the minutes
  • What have the last three budgets done to the water and sewer line, and what is the board projecting

The reserve study question is the one I would push hardest on. Reserve studies in this valley are thorough about the things that fail visibly, and an irrigation system is not always among them. An association with a twenty-five-year-old system, a master meter, and a tier four summer behind it has a cost coming that will not show up in the dues until it shows up all at once. That is a different risk from a roof, because a roof is on a schedule and a surcharge is not.

For buyers working from another state or another country, the practical difficulty is that these answers live in documents rather than in photographs, and they take time to collect. It is the same reason the process of buying here remotely runs on a longer timeline than buyers expect. A seller can produce a survey in a day. An association treasurer producing three years of budgets and a reserve study takes longer than that, and the request is better made in the first week of the contract than the last.

The reservoir at the top of Homestake Creek

There is a larger version of this story, and it is worth knowing about even though it will not change an offer this month. Aurora has applied for a state dredge-and-fill permit for Wild Horse Reservoir, a 95,000 acre-foot project in Park County that would be filled using water rights on Homestake Creek that Aurora has held since the 1950s. The application was issued July 29 and the public comment period closes October 5.

Asked how much Homestake water the project would actually move east in a wet year, Aurora declined to put a number on it, saying there are “many hydrological and operational variables” and that the reservoir would not fill in one or two years. Vicki Flynn, who runs the Eagle River Coalition, was less equivocal: “We don't have the water. Any out-of-basin diversion is 100% consumptive use.” The Eagle River Water and Sanitation District said it needed more information before commenting.

The detail I keep returning to is that Homestake Reservoir is the same reservoir the Upper Eagle Regional Water Authority draws on to augment its diversions under the call that has been in place since August. As of this month it was 57 percent empty. Green Mountain was 86 percent empty. Wild Horse will take years and will be argued by people with better credentials than mine, but the reservoir at the center of the argument is the one currently backstopping water service through the middle of this valley.

If I had to rank the two for somebody buying this fall, I would put the association's master meter well ahead of the filing in Park County. A permit application a hundred miles east is a ten-year question. An irrigation system on a single meter in a tier four summer is a June question, and it is the one that shows up in a budget you will be paying into.

I could be wrong about that ranking. If Wild Horse gets permitted and built, the people arguing about it this fall will turn out to have been the ones paying attention, and the rest of us will have been reading water bills. But a buyer choosing between two properties in October is not choosing on the strength of a reservoir that would take a decade to fill.

Who actually holds the answer

Three different offices hold pieces of this. The Division of Water Resources office in Glenwood Springs answers questions about permits and decrees. The water district answers questions about the tap, the meter, and the tier. A Colorado water attorney answers everything that turns out to be complicated, and on a ranch purchase that is most of it. What I can do is say which one to call first, and make sure the questions get asked during the inspection period rather than after closing, when the only remaining way to get an answer is to pay for it.

The Wild Horse comment period closes October 5. Comments go to the state health department at cdphe_df_wqcd@state.co.us with “WHS PN Comments” in the subject line.

This is general information about how water works in this valley rather than legal, engineering, or financial advice. Water rights, well permits, and augmentation plans are specific to a property, and the answers for one address can differ from the answers for another twenty minutes away. For a specific property, work with a Colorado water attorney, the Division of Water Resources, and the water provider that serves it.

Frequently Asked Questions

What does it mean that there is a call on the Eagle River?

A call is the state enforcing the order of water rights. Colorado allocates water by priority rather than by who owns the land, so every decreed right carries a date and the oldest rights are satisfied first. On August 14, 2026 the Colorado Division of Water Resources placed a call on the Eagle River after flows dropped below the 85 cubic feet per second required by a minimum streamflow decree dated October 7, 1981 for the reach between Gore Creek and Lake Creek. Junior water users in that stretch have to stop diverting or replace what they take with water from another source. Senior rights, which on this stretch include golf courses holding historic agricultural rights, continue to divert.

Do water rights come with the property when I buy a ranch in Eagle County?

Not automatically, and this is the single most expensive assumption a buyer can make. Water rights in Colorado are real property and can be sold separately from the land they once served. A deed conveying the property together with all appurtenant water rights sounds comprehensive but tells you very little on its own. What tells you something is the specific list: case numbers, decreed amounts, priority dates, the structure each right is tied to, and whether any right has been changed in water court for a use other than the one it was decreed for. Ditch company shares are separate again, because they are corporate stock transferred on the company books rather than through the deed.

What can I do with a household-use-only well permit in Colorado?

Less than most buyers expect. A household-use-only permit generally covers indoor use in one single-family home at up to 15 gallons per minute, with no outdoor lawn irrigation and no livestock watering. Most wells drilled on parcels under 35 acres since May 1972 fall into this category. If the plan for a property involves a garden, a pasture, or animals, the permit that allows those things is a different permit, and confirming which one a property actually holds is worth doing before the inspection period closes rather than after.

How many acres do I need in Colorado for a well that can irrigate and water livestock?

Generally 35 acres or more. A domestic exempt well on a parcel of at least 35 acres can typically serve up to three single-family dwellings, irrigate up to one acre of lawn and garden, and water domestic animals. Only one exempt well is permitted on the 35-acre portion. Below that acreage the options narrow considerably, and a lot inside a platted subdivision may not qualify for an exempt well at all, in which case it depends on being included in a court-decreed plan for augmentation.

What is a plan for augmentation and why does my subdivision have one?

A plan for augmentation is a water court decree that allows water to be used out of priority by replacing the depletion it causes with water from another source, usually storage. It is the mechanism that lets a subdivision keep its taps running during a call that would otherwise shut off a junior well. It is also a continuing obligation rather than a one-time approval: the replacement water has to come from somewhere, it costs money, and the cost recurs annually. On a subdivision lot that cost is usually carried by the homeowners association or the metropolitan district, which means it reaches the owner through dues rather than through a water bill.

Can my HOA raise dues or levy a special assessment because of water costs?

It can, and the governing documents and the Colorado Common Interest Ownership Act set out how. The more useful point for a buyer is where water costs enter an association budget in the first place. Many buildings and subdivisions here are a single account on a master meter, so common-area irrigation counts as one customer's usage. If that puts the association into the district's upper usage tiers, the resulting surcharge is billed to the association rather than to any individual owner, and it returns to owners through dues or, after a bad season, through an assessment. Augmentation obligations, irrigation system replacement, and landscape conversion work land the same way.

What are Stage III water restrictions in the Vail Valley?

The Eagle River Water and Sanitation District board declared a water shortage on April 9, 2026 and enacted Stage III restrictions under its Water Shortage Response Plan. Outdoor watering is limited to two days a week assigned by street address, and only between midnight and 8 a.m. or between 8 p.m. and midnight. Violations of the outdoor watering schedule carry fines, and a fourth violation within twelve months can result in a flow restricting device installed on the meter at the customer's expense. Separate surcharges apply to excessive use: a 15 percent surcharge in tier four and a flat 400 dollar fine plus escalating per-gallon charges in tier five.

What water questions should I ask before closing on a condo or HOA property in the Vail Valley?

Start with how the water arrives: district service, a shared central well, or individual wells. Then ask whether the association is master metered or individually metered and which usage tier it finished in last August. Ask whether the property sits inside a decreed plan for augmentation and what the association pays into it each year. Ask whether the reserve study covers the irrigation system or stops at roofs, asphalt, and paint. Ask whether the association has been surcharged or fined in the last two seasons and whether that appears in the minutes. Finally, read what the last three budgets did to the water and sewer line rather than only the current one.

Does the Aurora Wild Horse Reservoir project affect Vail Valley property owners?

Not in a way that changes an offer this month, but it is worth understanding. Aurora has applied for a state dredge-and-fill permit for Wild Horse Reservoir, a 95,000 acre-foot project in Park County that would be filled using Homestake Creek water rights Aurora has held since the 1950s. The application was issued July 29, 2026 and the public comment period closes October 5, 2026. Aurora has declined to say how much Homestake water would move east in a given year, citing hydrological and operational variables. The reason it matters locally is that Homestake Reservoir is the same reservoir the Upper Eagle Regional Water Authority draws on to augment its diversions under the current call.

Is the Vail Valley running out of water?

That is a bigger question than a real estate post can settle, and anyone who answers it confidently in either direction is overselling. What is verifiable is that Eagle County spent the summer of 2026 entirely in exceptional drought following the worst snowpack year on record, that the Eagle River has been under a call since August 14, that the district has been in a declared water shortage since April, and that Homestake Reservoir was 57 percent empty in September. What is also true is that the district and the authority have decreed water rights, storage, and augmentation supplies specifically so that service continues through years like this one. The practical consequence for a buyer is cost and restriction rather than an empty tap.
