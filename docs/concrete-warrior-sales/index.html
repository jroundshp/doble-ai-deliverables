Who Actually Buys This

A read on the sales list, on the odds of moving topsoil and concrete before the season closes, and on what the market is saying right now. The written season plan looks unlikely. A smaller real order book looks very reachable. Those are two different sentences and the distance between them is the subject.

The short answer

Where this comes out

Read this as a reforecast, not a warning. Nothing on this page says the yard cannot sell. Production is running ahead of its own schedule, the market turned upward in August after the slowdown we reported on 7 September, and there is more reachable demand inside the circle than the sheet currently shows. What looks unlikely is the number written on the plan. Those two things need saying in that order, because a room that hears the second one first stops listening to the rest.

Topsoil will sell. The season plan will not. The yard is producing ahead of schedule and there is a real buyer set inside the circle, but 93% of the season's revenue still has no named buyer against it, and the window for delivering topsoil that has to be seeded closes in about four weeks. Most of what is produced after that becomes stockpile, which is inventory rather than a cheque.

Concrete is the weaker line on today's evidence, and it is also the one with the clearest fix. The plant has been live since 24 August and has poured 20 yards, all of it used on site. The season plan is 10,100 yards. Nearly all of that rests on one relationship whose scope nobody has confirmed. But the reason concrete looks dead is partly that we have been looking in the wrong place, and the concrete section names eleven live jobs that prove it.

Put those three together and the list does not contain a hidden buyer. The accounts that can write a cheque before the ground freezes are the fifteen we already know about, and the constraint is not names. It is that two of them were met on 31 August and nobody recorded what happened.

And it is happening on our doorstep

We pulled the state’s live active drilling rig list this morning. Of the 34 rigs turning to the right in North Dakota today, 17 sit within roughly 50 straight-line miles of the yard, and 11 within about 35. The closest is a Hess well about ten miles out. Two Burlington Resources rigs are drilling a pad at Demicks Lake, on the Mandaree side of Fort Berthold, roughly 17 miles away. An XTO well is about 14 miles out, and XTO is ExxonMobil’s operating arm, which is the same company as one of the 31 August meetings.

Method, and its limit

Those distances are computed from the township and range in each well’s legal location, converted to a position with an accuracy of roughly ten miles. They are straight-line, not driving. Treat “17 of 34” as about half the state’s drilling is in the counties around us, which is the honest version, rather than as a count to quote at anybody.

Two corrections this pull forces

Phoenix Operating is not a Stanley story. Our sheet says they mean pad demand around Stanley. They went from one rig to three between July and August, the largest single operator increase in the state, and all three are in Divide and Burke counties, 52 to 59 miles out. Growing fast, in the wrong direction.

Burlington Resources is missing from the sheet entirely, and it is running two rigs on the same Fort Berthold pad, closer to the yard than almost anything else drilling in North Dakota.

What rigs mean, and what they do not

A rig means a new pad, and a new pad consumes scoria and aggregate for the pad and the lease road. It does not consume delivered topsoil, because the rule requires the operator to salvage eight inches of their own soil at construction and put it back at the end. Our topsoil market is the subset where that stockpile is short, lost or contaminated. The 411 inactive wells added last month are the better topsoil signal: a well going quiet is a reclamation job forming.

Topsoil

Production is fine. Sales are the problem.

Through 11 September the yard had produced 27,300 yards, which at its own $32 a yard is $873,600 of material, and Richard’s note of 10 September calls the first pile finished at that figure. Week two closed at 11,800 yards against a 10,000 plan, the season’s best week; week three ran 7,500 against the same plan. Nothing about the supply side is broken.

Jeff’s answer on 4 September is the one that matters: the 10,000 yards a week is a production figure, and sales are unknown at this time. Topsoil and concrete together carry $6,774,000 of the $7,299,000 season, and none of it has a named buyer. Unknown is not the same as unsold, and it should never be written as though it were. But it is also not an order book.

The calendar is the harder constraint

Topsoil that has to be seeded can only go down in two windows. The state’s own specification prefers an early spring seeding before 24 May and accepts a dormant fall seeding after 20 October. Spring is largely unusable because road load restrictions run from about early March to late May, so heavy loads cannot move through the preferred window. That makes autumn the real delivery season, and it opens on 20 October. Production stops around 15 November when the ground gets too wet to scrape.

So there are about four weeks in which topsoil for placement can both be produced and legally delivered. Material sold for stockpiling rather than immediate placement is not bound by that, and material already on the ground can keep moving for a couple of weeks after the trommels stop, which is the cheapest volume available and the least discussed.

What this means in plain terms

A stockpile is not a loss. It is next spring’s inventory, and pre-positioning material before the March load bans is a genuine advantage. But it is not cash, and the reason this workstream opened was that creditors are waiting. Those are two different problems and the plan currently treats them as one.

Concrete

We have been looking in the wrong place

The plant went live on 24 August. As of the 4 September log it had produced 20 yards, on one day, for internal use, with the dollar cell deliberately empty. The season plan is 10,100 yards at $300, which is 42% of the season’s revenue. Richard’s own read on the call was blunt and correct: it is the demand side, not the supply side. The plant can do 250 yards an hour. The forecast is 500 a week.

Two things explain the softness better than anything on the old sheet did.

The biggest pour in the circle finished in June. The 4 Bears Casino and Lodge expansion was a $100 million, seven-storey job with Kraus-Anderson as general contractor, and it completed this summer. Our sheet still carries Four Bears as a target. What is left on it is finish-out. That row should be downgraded.

And the sheet is 78 rows of oilfield. Contractors, operators, midstream, hydrovac, trucking. That is the right shape for topsoil and for pad aggregate. It is the wrong shape for ready-mix, because ready-mix on this reservation is bought by people putting up buildings.

The 10,000 yards attached to Jesse at Dark Horse is enormous for an RV park. Ten thousand cubic yards is a six-inch slab across about thirteen acres. The 29 August call also recorded that same job as the Woodstone road job, which raises the possibility that it is one job counted twice, or that the material is aggregate rather than concrete. Forty-two percent of the season’s revenue is resting on it. Confirm the scope, the unit and the pour schedule before anything else on the concrete side is planned. Treat that as a gate rather than an action item: until it is answered, nobody should be sitting in a meeting allocating plant time against it.

The gate nobody has checked

We are not on the tribe’s own vendor list

MHA TERO publishes six lists of who is licensed to work on Fort Berthold. Across all of them there are about 1,058 companies. Concrete Warrior is not on any of them.

The Certified Indian Contractor list, which is the one a Native-owned firm would appear on, was last reissued on 17 June 2026. Our own record has a TERO licence confirmed by Breanna on 6 August, which is after that date. So there are two possible explanations and they need different actions: either the licence exists and the published list is simply stale, or it does not appear where a buyer would look. Either way, a TERO-compliant buyer checking today does not find us. Desiree Conklin is the chief licensing specialist and is the person who settles it.

Meanwhile the incumbent is listed, and listed for exactly our work

Strata Corporation, which is Knife River, holds MHA TERO Prime General Contractor licence 27-9089, valid to 29 April 2027. Its registered scope, in the tribe’s own words, reads construction, hazmat/reclamation, material supplier. The competitor has reclamation and material supply written onto a current licence on the reservation, and we do not.

Five more licensed material suppliers sit on those same lists: Gratech (earthwork, aggregates, asphalt, currently tagged to a job on ND-23), Fisher Sand & Gravel, Sundre Sand & Gravel, Badlands Aggregate, and Dickinson Ready Mix, which holds an approved oilfield vendor licence for the sale of concrete products. Dickinson is about 130 miles away, so freight is still ours to win. But they are listed and we are not.

This is not the same thing as ISNetworld

ISNetworld, Veriforce and Avetta are the operator-side prequalification gates and they take at least thirty days. That work still matters and Breanna started it. The TERO listing is different: it is the tribe’s gate, it governs the tribal and public jobs named earlier, and it is the one where being in New Town is supposed to be the advantage.

Argue with this

What would change our mind

Each of these would move an odd above by a lot, and each is a question for the people who were in the room rather than something we can check from here.

  • The 31 August meetings went well and there is a verbal commitment nobody wrote down. This is the most likely single reason our topsoil read is too pessimistic.
  • The Dark Horse yardage is real, is concrete, and pours in October. Odds 03 goes from roughly 1 in 8 to better than even on that alone.
  • A large share of the topsoil is already being taken by a buyer we have not been told about. Jeff’s “unknown” means unknown to the sheet, not unsold. Somebody may simply know.
  • Richard reads the eleven tribal jobs and says the mix specification or the haul rules them out. Entirely possible. He would know in a glance, and it would kill the concrete argument cleanly.
  • The TERO licence does exist and the published list is just behind. Likely, and it would turn the TERO listing from a gap into a five-minute administrative fix.

Method and limits

What is sourced and what is ours

Sourced and checkable: every rig count, price, permit, completion and well figure comes from the state Director’s Cut of 20 August 2026 or the live DMR active drilling rig list pulled on 10 September. Every licence number, contact, phone, email, scope and expiry date comes from the MHA TERO lists dated 3 September 2026 and 17 June 2026. Every production and plan figure comes from Jeff’s own workbook and daily log.

Ours, and offered as judgement: the four odds, the reading of the three colours, and the conclusion that the concrete pipeline should be tribal vertical construction rather than oilfield. These are arguments, not findings.

Approximate: rig distances, as set out under the market heading. About ten miles of error, straight-line rather than driving.

Deliberately not here: margins, unit economics and any figure that should not travel outside the ownership group. Nothing on this page is written to be forwarded to a customer.
