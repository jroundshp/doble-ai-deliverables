Costs are already tight, so the whole gap has to come from the top line. This page starts by making $6,000 a week concrete, then argues about where it can realistically come from.

It comes in two parts. Part one is where the growth is: four lanes that add up to the number. Part two is what turned up while checking the menus and prices to build part one, and it is here because several of those things are holding sales down today.

$6,000 more in weekly sales across the bake shop and the bistro.
The short version

Ninety seconds, then the detail.

$6,000 a week is 667 sourdough loaves, 1,143 lattes, or 429 Greek salads. Nobody sells that across a counter that shuts at half past two. So this is not a traffic problem. It is a capacity and channel problem, and both businesses have unsold capacity in plain sight.

Four lanes get to the number:

  1. Stop closing early Thursday to Saturday. Bar only. Mostly an extension of nights already open.$2,000
  2. Sell bread wholesale. Starting with Holiday Valley, Trattoria Partenope and his own bistro.$1,500
  3. Book the rooms. Three private spaces that have never published a price.$1,500
  4. Lift the ticket. Forty eight cents on every order, and gift cards people can find.$1,000

The single biggest one: on Friday the bistro shuts at eight while the Gin Mill runs to eleven, and on Saturday it shuts at nine while Balloons runs to two in the morning. Add Thursday, the night the village's evening rooms open their week, and that is eleven hours a week of trade walking past a fifty seat bourbon bar. Two of those three nights are already open and already staffed, so most of this is staying later rather than opening new. Sunday to Wednesday stays shut, because outside holiday weeks the town is quiet and the operators who know it best are closed too.

Ten things are already built and not switched on, among them a loyalty program the bake shop runs and never mentions, gift cards no page links to, and a bakery three blocks from a bistro whose entire bread range is one bagel.

One structural decision sits under all of it. The bake shop runs on Toast and the bistro runs on Square, so a single gift card and a single loyalty account across both shops is currently impossible. That is a project, not a switch, and it is worth starting the conversation now.

Everything below is the working. Part one is where the growth is. Part two is what turned up while checking the menus and prices to build part one. Two numbers from Corey would sharpen all of it: tickets and average ticket per shop, and why the evenings are closed.

In winter the mountain a mile away keeps its lifts running five to six hours past the bistro's closing time. Night skiing starts at 3:30, half an hour before the bistro locks up, and runs to mid March. That is a seasonal argument rather than a year round one, and it is worth treating as the upside case rather than the base case.

But the village does not answer that demand every night, and it matters which nights it does. The two venues closest in kind to Alpine Roots both run four nights, not seven. The Banq opens Wednesday to Saturday and is dark Sunday, Monday and Tuesday. Villaggio opens Thursday to Sunday and is dark Monday to Wednesday. Only the big anchors, the Gin Mill and Ellicottville Brewing, carry seven nights, and they have the volume to justify it.

That is the real shape of this town. Outside holiday weeks, Sunday through Wednesday nights are thin and the operators who know the market do not fight it. So the lane is not five nights. It is Thursday, Friday and Saturday.

And here is the part that makes it easy: on two of those three nights the bistro is already open and already staffed. It simply closes early. Friday it shuts at eight while the Gin Mill runs to eleven. Saturday it shuts at nine while Balloons runs to two in the morning. Thursday it shuts at four, which is the night the village's evening rooms start their week.

The uncomfortable part is what those two four-night rooms actually are. The Gin Mill's Carriage House is a small private bourbon bar with sixty five bourbons. The Banq is a speakeasy style cocktail lounge. Those are Alpine Roots and the cocktail room, already built, already trading, four blocks away, on exactly the nights Corey is closed.

Corey owns a fifty seat bourbon bar, a fifteen seat cocktail room, a wine list and a liquor license, and on the three nights that matter it is either locked by four or closes two to three hours before everybody else. Bar revenue is also the best margin in the building. A $15 glass of Cabernet does not carry the labor or the food cost of a $13.99 salad, which means evening dollars close the gap faster than counter dollars of the same size.

04  /  Already paid for

Ten things they own and are not selling.

Before any of the bigger lanes, there is a shorter list worth walking through, because everything on it already exists. It has been built, paid for and staffed. It is simply not switched on, not signposted, or buried where nobody scrolls.

This is the cheapest revenue in the business, and most of it can be done in an afternoon by one person.

01 A fifty seat bourbon bar

Alpine Roots exists, seats fifty, and appears nowhere in the bistro's site navigation. It is described in a single FAQ answer near the bottom of the About page and nowhere else on the site, and it is shut on the nights the village drinks.

02 A loyalty program

The bake shop already awards a point per dollar. It is on no sign, no menu, no website and no receipt that could be found from outside. A running rewards scheme with no promotion is the cheapest repeat-visit mechanism they have.

03 Gift cards

Alpine Bistro sells Square eGift cards at $10, $20, $25, $50 and custom. Not one page on either website links to them. The bake shop has none at all.

04 A bundle that works

"The Board and Brew," a sandwich plus a 16 oz mocha for $14.25, with its own artwork. It is the only cross-sell product either business sells, and it sits at the bottom of a category nobody scrolls to.

05 A group product

A 96 oz coffee carafe at $32.25 with cups, lids, sugars and creamers. Exactly what a ski bus, a lacrosse team or a Holiday Valley conference room buys, and it is unpromoted.

06 Three private rooms

Bourbon bar, cocktail room and cafe, all described on the events page, none with a price, a minimum or a deposit. Every prospect has to call to find out whether they can afford it.

07 A wholesale program

The page exists. It has no form, no product list, no prices and no minimums, in a village where every restaurant buys bread from somebody.

08 A bakery three blocks away

The bistro's entire bread range is one $3.00 bagel that stops selling at 2:29pm. The bake shop closes at 2:30. From half past two nobody in the village can buy a loaf, while the bistro trades on for hours.

09 An apartment upstairs

Alpine Overlook already sends its guests into both shops. Nothing sends shop customers back the other way, and no guest leaves an email address behind.

10 A Taste of Ellicottville award

Alpine Bistro won Healthy Choice at the town's own food festival on the weekend of 8 August, announced by the chamber. It is not on either homepage, and the only public record found is a post somebody else wrote. A town that just voted for you is the cheapest endorsement there is, and it belongs on the door, the menu, the receipt and the counter.

Not one of these needs a budget, a vendor or a new product. Every one of them needs somebody to be given the job.

Book the rooms

The events page already lists three spaces and a full catering menu, and it never states a price, a minimum or a deposit. Every prospect has to call to learn whether they can afford it, and most will not. Publishing terms converts browsers into bookings without adding a single seat.

Three ways to price it, and all three work: a flat room fee, which is simplest and reads as fair. No room charge against a minimum spend, which feels generous to the guest and protects the night for the house. Or a hybrid, a smaller fee with a lower minimum, which is safest on a marquee weekend. Pick one per room and per season, and put the number on the page.

06  /  The mountain

Three ways into Holiday Valley.

Holiday Valley is a mile up the road and it is the reason most of the traffic exists. It already sends people to both businesses: the resort's own Village Options dining page names Alpine Bistro and Ellicottville Bake Shop. That is a warm relationship to trade on rather than a cold call.

Wholesale Sell them the one thing they cannot buy

The resort is not short of a bakery vendor, and it will not switch its whole bread program for a village shop. But its lodge coffee and pastry counter runs on Starbucks and a broadline supplier, and that supplier does not make handmade sourdough a mile down the hill. Do not pitch a bread contract. Pitch one or two signature items that Holiday Valley cannot source anywhere else and can put a local name on.

Open with: the Ellicottville sourdough loaf and one signature pastry, named as local on their menu.
Three doors, not one: lodge food service  ·  the Cabana Bar  ·  the conference and banquet program, which buys bread by the tray and books months ahead.

Buses Get the coach to stop in the village first

Chartered ski buses run to Holiday Valley all winter from Cleveland, Toronto and the Buffalo suburbs. Those coaches arrive with forty to fifty people who have been on a bus for two or three hours and have not eaten. Right now they roll straight past Washington Street to the base lodge. A fifteen minute coffee and pastry stop on the way in is a standing pre-order, not a walk-in gamble.

Named operators to approach: Chagrin Valley Ski Club (Cleveland, four chartered Sunday buses a season)  ·  High Park Ski Club (Toronto, multi-day coach trips)  ·  Suburban Ski Club  ·  Fullington Trailways, which runs a scheduled Buffalo to Ellicottville service daily.
The offer: a fixed per-head coffee and pastry package, pre-ordered and pre-paid by the trip organizer, bagged and ready when the coach pulls in.

Colleges Follow the student nights

Holiday Valley prices a college night lift ticket at $39 Sunday through Thursday, runs a College Daze weekend package out of The Inn, and university ski clubs bus in on Friday nights. That is a large, organized, repeat audience that arrives on a schedule somebody publishes in advance. Reaching thirty club officers is a far cheaper way to fill a room than reaching thirty thousand strangers. This one does not depend on opening extra nights, because a booked club night fills a private room on a date the group picks, which is usually the Friday or Saturday they are already in town.

Who to contact: ski and snowboard club officers at Buffalo, Canisius, St. Bonaventure, RIT, Pitt, Toronto and the Cleveland area schools, plus Holiday Valley's own group sales desk.
The offer: a fixed price bus package, and the bourbon bar or cocktail room booked out for a club night, which is exactly the private room product already sitting on the events page.

These three lanes all feed the same fact: the demand is already organized into groups, with a named person who books for the group. Group revenue is forecastable in a way that walk-in revenue never is.

07  /  The calendar

Every event on the calendar gets a plan.

Ellicottville publishes its own demand schedule a year in advance, and it is not only the ski season. Two resorts, a chamber, a lacrosse festival and a bike park all put dated, ticketed, organized traffic into a four block village. Right now each of those weekends is treated as weather: it arrives, the shops get busy, nothing was prepared for it.

The initiative is to work the calendar rather than react to it. One named owner, one page per event, and a standing rhythm: four weeks out decide the offer, two weeks out post and email it, the week of it is on the counter and in the windows.

08  /  Who owns this

Somebody has to hold the whole picture.

Nearly everything in this document is a small job that nobody currently has. Price the bread on the menu. Reset the shelf in the morning. Link the gift card. Answer on Airbnb. Call Trattoria about bread. Individually they are afternoons. Collectively they are a role.

An extra $6,000 a week is $312,000 a year. A target that size does not get hit by a list of tidy-ups shared out among people who each own a slice. It needs one person who can see across the bakery, the bistro, the bar, the rooms and the apartment at once, who owns the process and is accountable for the result. That is worth an honest look at how the Ellicottville team is structured before any of the lanes get started.

Underneath that sits the cheapest lever in the building. Every idea here dies at the counter if the person working it does not know about it. A customer asks the barista about a birthday party and gets a shrug. Somebody buying four loaves is never asked whether their restaurant wants a standing order. A family checking out of the apartment is never told there is a bourbon bar downstairs.

The fix is a standing ten minute huddle, weekly or every other week, at both shops. Not a meeting about the schedule. A meeting about what we sell.

□ The whole menu, both shops

Every person at the bake shop should be able to describe what the bistro does, and the reverse. They are one customer base in a four block village.

□ The event spaces

Three rooms, capacities, who to hand the enquiry to, and a card to give out. Every staff member should know this cold.

□ The apartment upstairs

What it sleeps, what it costs, where to send someone who asks. It is directly above the bistro and most guests do not know it exists.

□ The wholesale program

Anyone buying in volume is a lead. Staff need one sentence and a contact to hand over.

□ This week's add-on

One item, one line, everybody says it. This is the mechanic behind the $0.48 a ticket in lane four.

□ What is coming

The next event on the calendar and what we are doing about it, so the staff hear it before the customers do.

09  /  The apartment

The cross sell only runs one way.

Alpine Overlook already does the hard part. Guests get breakfast downstairs and a $20 bakery credit, so every stay pushes traffic into both shops. It works, it is rated 5.0, and it is a genuine advantage no other rental in the village has.

What is missing is the return trip. Nothing flows the other direction. The bake shop has hundreds of customers a day and none of them are told there is an apartment above the bistro. Guests leave without an email address on file, so a family that came for Fall Festival never hears about a February weekend. And a guest who spends the $20 credit and nothing more is a cost, not a channel.

Three fixes: capture the guest email at booking and run one seasonal offer a year, put a card in the room and a sign at both registers pointing each way, and set the credit high enough that guests walk out having spent past it.

Part two

What we found along the way.

None of this was what we set out to look for. It surfaced while checking prices and menus to build the numbers above. It is here because several of these are holding sales down today, and because a few of them are quick.

10  /  The one structural decision

Two shops, two systems, three blocks apart.

What the split actually costs

The bake shop runs on Toast. The bistro runs on Square. Two tills, two apps, two back offices, two sets of reports, two logins, for one owner with two shops three blocks apart. Both are closed systems, so this is not a branding quibble, it is a wall:

Gift cards
A Square card sold at the bistro cannot be spent at the bake shop, and the bake shop has no card at all
Loyalty
Points earned at the bake shop do not exist at the bistro, so the customer has to care about two schemes to be rewarded for one relationship
Customers
Two separate databases, so nobody can see that the person buying bread every Saturday is also the one booking the apartment
Pricing
Two catalogues maintained by hand, which is why a sticky bun costs $1.25 more four blocks away

One card and one account spendable at both counters is the single most valuable thing these two businesses could share, and it is the one thing the current setup makes impossible. Getting there means picking one platform and moving the other onto it. That is a real project rather than a switch: menus rebuilt, staff retrained, hardware swapped, contracts timed. It does not belong on the list below. It belongs on a decision list, and the longer it waits the more history has to be migrated.

What works in the meantime, and needs no integration at all: make each store carry the other's range. A customer browsing the bistro menu should be able to see the sourdough, the honey wheat and the stuffed focaccia, with the price, the photograph and a plain line saying it is collected three blocks away at the bake shop, and a link straight to it. The checkout stays separate. The knowing does not have to. Right now somebody on the bistro site has no way to learn that a scratch bakery under the same ownership is a three minute walk away, and that is a discovery problem rather than a technology one. Run the same trick in reverse: the bake shop's store should show the bistro's bowls, flatbreads and the bourbon bar. Add a paper cross-shop offer honoured at both counters and you have most of the benefit of one system, for the price of an afternoon.

It is also worth asking how this happened, because the answer usually decides how hard it is to undo. Two systems three blocks apart is rarely a decision anybody made. It is normally what is left over when two businesses were set up at different times by different people, and nobody has since been given the job of choosing.

11  /  Where the numbers disagree

Three brands, four sets of hours, five price lists.

There are three separate web presences here, not two. alpinebistrony.com, ellicottvillebakeshop.com, and ellicottvillecoffee.com, which is the Square store the bistro's Order Online button opens. The third one carries its own name, its own email address, its own hours and its own story, and it lists the same street address as the bistro.

Between them these sites publish prices in five places and opening hours in four, and they do not agree.

The clearest problem is the barista menu. Every drink on the 2024 PDF is fifty cents to a dollar behind what the tills actually charge, and that file is still linked from the bake shop website today. A customer deciding on their phone is reading last year's prices.

The pattern on hours is worse, because there is no pattern. Two sites publish different closing times for the same address.

Tidy-ups, none of which cost anything.

□ Price the bread on the menu

Toast has every loaf priced. The PDF the website links to has none of them. Copy the prices across so the menu people actually read is complete.

□ Repost the barista menu

The coffee menu on the site is the 2024 file and every drink on it is fifty cents to a dollar behind the till.

□ Check the 45 minute wait

The ordering page quotes 40 to 45 minutes for pickup. If that is a stale default it is quietly turning away every quick order.

□ Cross-list both menus

Show the bakery's loaves on the bistro store and the bistro's bowls on the bakery store, each marked "collect at the other shop" with a link. Separate checkouts, one visible range.

□ Reset the shelf each morning

Seven items were struck out at 7:10am on a Tuesday, before opening. Marking the day's bake back in should be somebody's named opening task.

□ Stock the online store

Appetizers, Mediterranean Lunch and Flatbread Pizzas sit in the catalog but never appear in the store menu. The website sells them, the store will not.

□ Fix the catering PDF

The email field on the live catering menu prints the phone number instead of an email address.

□ Reconcile the hours

Four published closing times across three sites. Pick the true ones, put them everywhere, and match Google.

□ Answer on Airbnb

Response rate is showing 67%. Airbnb demotes listings below roughly 90% in search, so this is costing bookings quietly.

□ Fix the third bedroom

The listing sells three bedrooms but the sleeping section shows a bed in only two. A family of eight comparing options will skip it.

alpinebistrony.com/fr/ serves English text under a French language tag. Given how much Ontario traffic the village gets, translate it properly or take it down.

Keep Ellicottville Coffee as a real brand and give it its own story, or fold it into Alpine Bistro. Either is fine. Leaving it half-alive with another company's About page is not. Related to, but separate from, the Toast and Square decision above.

14  /  How people find them

The visitor with a phone.

Everything above this is about people who are already in the village. This section is about the ones still deciding whether to walk down the street.

Somebody arrives in Ellicottville for a weekend and knows nothing about it. They do not know there is a bakery three blocks from a bourbon bar, and they are not going to walk the length of Washington Street to find out. They take out a phone and they type. Sourdough. Best coffee. Somewhere for breakfast. A bar that is open now.

That search is the front door to every lane in this document. The later evenings, the private rooms, the wholesale bread and the ticket lift all assume somebody walks in. The two websites are what answers that search, and today they answer almost none of it.

What a visitor typesWhat the two sites give that search
best coffee in EllicottvilleThe bistro's homepage is titled "Alpine Bistro | Coffee and Pastries" and does not contain the word Ellicottville. The address printed on that same page reads "16 Washington Street, NY 14731," with the town left out.
sourdough EllicottvilleThe word sourdough appears once across both websites, as body text on the bakery's menu page. It is in no page title, no heading and no description. For a bakery whose own story is about bread, that is the whole business sitting in the one place a search engine weighs least.
bourbon bar near Holiday ValleyNeither site contains the words Holiday Valley or HoliMont anywhere, on any page. Those two resorts are the reason nearly every visitor is in town.
is the bakery open right nowBoth sites print their hours for a person to read. Neither states them in a form a search engine or an assistant can read, so neither can be answered with "open until half past two."
gluten free EllicottvilleThe words gluten and vegan appear nowhere on either site. The bistro's FAQ does answer allergies, in prose, which serves a reader and is invisible to everything else.
coffee shop with wifiThe bistro answers this one, and answers it well. That is the next paragraph, and it is the point.

The heart of it They already wrote the answers.

The bistro's About page carries a genuine FAQ. It answers the hours, whether the place is open year round, indoor and outdoor seating, whether you need a reservation, food allergies, the coffee range, what Alpine Roots is, whether there is Wi-Fi, and whether dogs are welcome. Somebody sat down and answered the exact questions a visitor asks before walking in, and answered them properly.

Then it was filed on a page called About Us, with no description, and with nothing anywhere on the site marking it as a set of questions and answers. A person who lands on that page gets everything they need. A search engine sees a page called About Us. The work is done and paid for. It is stored where nobody is looking.

That is the same pattern as section 04, and it is worth saying plainly: this is not a content problem. It is a wiring problem.

What is actually missing, checked page by page. Neither site carries a description on a single page. Neither tells a machine what kind of business it is, where it is, or when it opens. Across both sites combined there are roughly 2,500 words for the bistro and 3,000 for the bakery, which is very little for a search engine to work with and almost nothing for an AI assistant to quote.

□ Bistro  ·  Give every page a description

Six pages, no meta descriptions. This is the sentence Google prints under the link, and with none written Google invents one from whatever text it finds first.

□ Bistro  ·  Say what the business is

There is no structured data on any page of alpinebistrony.com. Nothing states that it is a cafe, where it is, or what hours it keeps. This is the single item that most affects whether an assistant can recommend it.

□ Bistro  ·  Put the town in the title

The homepage title and the homepage address both omit Ellicottville. Every competing search result in the village includes it.

□ Bistro  ·  Mark the FAQ as an FAQ

The answers are written. Tagging them so machines recognize them as questions and answers is a settings change, not new writing.

□ Bistro  ·  Turn pinch to zoom back on

The site switches off zoom on phones. For a cafe whose customers are mostly reading a menu on a phone, that is a daily irritation and an accessibility failure.

□ Bistro  ·  Make shared links show something

The site has no preview tags, so a link pasted into a text message or onto Facebook arrives as a bare address with no picture and no title. Every share the staff and customers make is worth less than it should be.

□ Bakery  ·  The homepage needs a headline

ellicottvillebakeshop.com has no H1 at all. The main heading is the one line every search engine reads first, and the page does not have one.

□ Bakery  ·  Structured data that describes a bakery

The site does emit structured data, but it describes a website rather than a business. Nothing in it says bakery, and nothing carries the address or the opening hours.

□ Bakery  ·  Put the bread where it counts

Sourdough, baguette, rye and focaccia are all on the menu page and none of them appear in a title or a heading. The products people search for by name should be findable by name.

□ Bakery  ·  Retitle the menu page

It is still called "EBS Menu 2024" while the menu it links to is dated January 2026. Anyone who finds it in search sees a two year old label.

□ Bakery  ·  Describe the photographs

Twelve of the thirteen images on the homepage have no alt text, so the pictures of the bread are invisible to image search and to anyone using a screen reader.

□ Both  ·  Name the mountains

Neither site mentions Holiday Valley or HoliMont. A page that says how far it is to each, and what time it opens relative to the lifts, answers a question thousands of visitors ask every season.

Two cautions.
