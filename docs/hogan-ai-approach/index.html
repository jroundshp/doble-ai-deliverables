AI across the portfolio

What it means for businesses like these, why an owner would do it, and the process we would follow.

Prepared for Corey Hogan Discussion draft · September 4, 2026 Link-shared, not indexed

The short version

The goal is to develop and increase business across all of these entities. There are nine of them, spanning industries with almost nothing to do with one another: a custom fiberglass manufacturer, a law firm, a bakery, restaurants in two states, a real estate brokerage. No single program fits a range that wide, and anybody who says otherwise has not looked at it closely.

What they do have in common is narrower, and more useful. Every job, every matter and every order passes through a stretch of digital work before anybody touches anything physical. That stretch is where each of them backs up, and it is the part AI is genuinely good at today. The plant, the dining room, the courtroom and the yard are people, and they stay people.

So the question is not which tool to buy. It is where the paperwork sits in each business, how much of it repeats, and what a first draft would have to look like for the person who owns that work to trust it. This page is that method, and VPC is where it gets tested first.

01 Where this picks up

This did not start in August, and the push did not come from us. It came from the top, with a reason attached: a company that does not take advantage of this gets left behind, financially and not only figuratively, while the ones that moved get further ahead.

On August 14 the group met on AI across the portfolio. That morning opened with a concession worth repeating, which is that everybody in the room can already open Claude and the tool was never the constraint. What has not existed since is a written answer to the obvious next question. This is that document. It is a method rather than a plan, and it is meant to be argued with.

02 Two halves of one program

Two things are going on, and they serve the same goal.

One. The websites need to be optimized, searchable and discoverable, and to work as a tool that increases sales rather than as a brochure that sits there.

Two. AI belongs inside the work itself, taking the grinding tasks so that people spend their hours on the parts that actually need a person.

The first decides whether a buyer ever arrives. The second decides what happens to them when they do. Neither is sufficient on its own, and that is the part most easily missed: a company nobody can find gets no benefit from answering quickly, and a company that is easy to find but slow to answer loses the enquiry it just won. Treated as separate projects competing for the same attention, they both stall. Treated as one program with two halves, each one makes the other worth more.

This page is about the second half. The first has its own workstream and its own people, and it is running alongside this rather than waiting behind it.

03 What this software actually is

Worth being plain about, because the word carries a lot of noise and the noise makes it harder to decide anything. Strip away the marketing and it is sophisticated software that reads and writes. It takes messy input, a rambling email, a scanned drawing, a spec section, a pile of invoices, and produces structured output: a summary, a draft, a filled-in form, an answer. It is very good at turning a blank page into a first version. It is not good at being right without anybody checking.

That is the whole design of everything downstream. The value is the distance between a blank page and a draft worth correcting, and the correction stays with a person.

What it does well today

  • Reads input that arrives in any shape and pulls the facts out of it.
  • Drafts a first version of something repetitive: a quote, a spec, a letter, a listing, a reply.
  • Finds things inside large piles of documents faster than a person can.
  • Answers the same question a hundred times from material that already exists.
  • Explains its own reasoning well enough that a person can check it.

What it does badly, and will for a while

  • Being reliably correct with nobody reviewing the output.
  • Anything where being wrong is expensive and no one is looking.
  • Judgment that depends on knowing a particular customer or a particular job.
  • Physical work of any kind.
  • Knowing what it does not know, which is why the human stays.

None of that requires a company to buy anything unusual, and none of it is the robot story. It is closer to hiring a very fast, very literal assistant who has read everything the company has written down and needs their work checked every time.

04 Where it helps, and how you find it

In every business here there is physical work that makes the money, and digital work sitting in front of it deciding how much of the physical work gets done.

That is the opening. Finding it in a particular business takes four questions, asked about the work rather than about the technology, in this order because each is cheaper to answer than the next.

  1. Does it repeat? Not whether it is hard or important. One-off work is the worst place to start, however painful it is.
  2. Does one pass cost real time? Minutes of skilled attention times how often. Three hours twenty times a year matters; four seconds a thousand times does not. That arithmetic is the whole reason a fiberglass quote and a coffee order lead to different answers.
  3. Can it be graded? Is there a record of the work done properly before, complete enough to mark a draft against. This kills more candidates than the other three combined.
  4. Are the records allowed to move? Client files, government drawings and customer data carry rules that come before any of this.

Anything surviving all four is worth a test. Anything failing the third gets parked, not because it does not matter but because nobody could tell whether it worked.

Draft and grade is the method this page describes: reconstruct finished work, write the option tree, have the software draft against it, and let the people who did the original grade the result. It fits where one pass through the digital work costs hours of skilled time against a large transaction.

Forecast and schedule is a different instrument for a different arithmetic. No single restaurant order is worth drafting, but there are thousands, and the value sits in patterns across the pile: what sells on which day, what to prepare, when to staff, what to order. Real work, and AI is good at it. Applying this method to it would waste everybody's time.

The diversity is not the obstacle

A portfolio can span businesses far more different than these, rockets and a social network and tunnelling and cars, and still have AI woven into every one of them. Those applications have nothing in common with each other. What they share is the commitment and the discipline: find the work that repeats, draft against work already finished, have a person grade it. What transfers between businesses is never the application. It is the method for finding it. That is why this page is a method rather than a program, and why a bakery being nothing like a fiberglass shop is a fact to design around rather than a reason to do nothing.

Written from what those companies do in public. We have not looked inside any of them and make no claim about how they run.

One row sits outside both columns. Patriot Stone and the North Dakota operation are taking real time, energy and money right now and belong in any honest picture of what is going on. They are not formally part of the portfolio, and they are not where AI integration should start. Listed, and then set aside.

What this is not

  • Not plant automation, and not robots.
  • Not the marketing and findability work. That is the other half of the same program and it runs alongside this, not instead of it.
  • Not a tool purchase, and not a vendor bake-off.
  • Not a replacement for the people doing the work. They are the control on the whole thing.

05 Why an owner would do this

Four reasons, and they are not the same reason in different clothes. The order is deliberate.

ReasonWhat it actually buys
A. More business through the same doorsThe queue stops deciding how much work gets taken on. A custom shop does not wait on the chopper gun and a firm does not wait on the courtroom; what sets the number sits upstream and is made of files. Hiring still happens.
B. The cost of being latePosition in the pack. Companies at scale are pointing this at digital work first, and the rest follow on a delay. A business that sits it out does not stay where it is, and the gap compounds in both directions.
C. The signal to buyersSpecifying engineers, OEMs, government and clients choosing a firm are all being asked the same AI question inside their own walls. A company that can describe a real process, a first draft and a human sign-off reads as one worth bringing into a bid. For these buyers the story leads with control, not with tools.
D. What you keep if it failsA written map of how the business actually prices and decides, which today lives in the heads of the people who quote by hand. This is the one usually left out, and it is what makes the first step nearly risk free.

The purpose is a more capable company, not a cheaper one on paper.

06 The process

This is what happens after a candidate survives all four questions. Six steps, and the sequence matters more than the speed. Two of them are places the whole thing is allowed to stop.

  1. Sit in the work. Time alongside the people doing it, on live jobs. What arrives, who chases what, what waits on whom. Not a survey.
  2. Reconstruct the last ten finished jobs in one lane: the inbound artifact as it arrived, the hours, the output, the price, and what changed between first version and final. Ground truth for everything after it.
  3. Write the option tree from the business's own material. Its packages, its forms, its language. A generic industry model would say nothing about this company.
  4. Blind test. Draft against those same ten without seeing the outcome. The people who did the original work grade it. The grade is theirs.
  5. Human sign-off, always. Nothing auto-sends, and nothing reaches a customer unattended.
  6. Live work only after the closed-job test holds. One lane, then widen.

Choosing the lane, and one honest complication

Pick on volume and repeat rate, out of what step 1 shows rather than whichever example is most interesting to talk about. The complication: the lane clogging the queue and the lane best suited to a first test are not always the same. A backup can come from the most bespoke work in the shop, which is the worst place to grade a draft. Where they diverge, test where the tree is narrow enough to grade and say so.

07 How anyone would know it worked

Measured against each business's own baselines, which exist inside its own files, and never against a generic industry model. The things worth counting are the same everywhere: hours of skilled time per first draft, time from a complete request to a first output, hit rate against the finished work used as the yardstick, rework after the first pass, and work that previously sat waiting.

Every one of those is deliberately blank right now. We do not have the baselines, and a number invented here would poison the comparison later.

One caution, because it has already come up. Some of these baselines do not exist anywhere. If nobody in a business can say what share of pricing requests turn into sales, that is not a gap in our access. It is a finding, and it is worth as much as anything the drafting produces.

08 Before any file moves

Each business carries a different constraint on its own records, and the constraint comes before the request rather than after it. VPC's finished jobs are for municipal utilities, industrial plants, the Navy and the Coast Guard, which makes those drawings the customer's business as much as VPC's. A law firm's files carry privilege. The hospitality businesses hold customer and payroll data.

So the rule is the same everywhere. Files stay inside the company's control, nothing goes into a public model or into anyone's training data, and how records are handled gets written down and agreed before the first one moves. The first document to read is not our policy but the business's own contract and professional obligations, because government agreements and bar rules can dictate how material is stored and who may see it. If a lane's paperwork cannot clear that, the test moves to a lane whose paperwork can.

09 VPC first

One business, one lane.

VPC is the right first one, and not because it is the largest. The digital work there is unusually well defined, the people who own it are identifiable, and the business has a record of executing on a written document without needing a meeting first. Quoting and submittals is the lane. There is a fuller brief on it, and the working session on September 9 is where the step 1 questions actually get asked.

And VPC is not a one-off. What comes out of it is reusable everywhere after it: the four questions, the option-tree format, the grading rubric, and a file-handling agreement already tested against government contracts. Business two starts from those rather than a blank page. That is why doing one properly beats starting nine.

A second business needs the same first step and nothing more: time with the people who own the paperwork, and ten finished jobs. No software, no commitment, and a real answer inside a couple of weeks.
