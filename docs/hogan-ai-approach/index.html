AI across the portfolio

What it means for businesses like these, why an owner would do it, and the process we would follow.

Prepared for Corey Hogan Discussion draft · September 4, 2026 Link-shared, not indexed

The short version

Nine businesses, and not one of them is a software company. What they have in common is that every job, every matter and every order passes through a stretch of digital work before anybody touches anything physical. That stretch is where each of these businesses backs up, and it is the part AI is genuinely good at today. The plant, the dining room, the courtroom and the yard are people, and they stay people. The question is not which tool to buy. It is where the paperwork sits in each business, how much of it repeats, and what a first draft would have to look like for the person who owns that work to trust it. This page states the method. VPC is where it gets tested first.

01 Where this picks up

On August 14 the group met on AI across the portfolio. That morning opened with a concession worth repeating, which is that everybody in the room can already open Claude and the tool was never the constraint. Then everyone ran the same buyer-intent searches from their own phones and looked at the results together.

What has not existed since is a written answer to the obvious next question. This is that document. It is a method, not a plan, and it is meant to be argued with.

02 What this actually means

Set the tools aside for a moment. In every business in this portfolio there is physical work that makes the money and digital work that sits in front of it deciding how much of the physical work gets done.

At VPC a request arrives in whatever form the customer felt like sending, a dimension is missing, somebody chases it, somebody draws, somebody prices. Three plants in Western New York, and how many jobs move through them is decided upstream of all three in a queue made of email, PDFs and drawings. The same shape shows up everywhere else in the portfolio, in different clothes. Intake and document assembly at a law firm. Ordering, scheduling and the paperwork behind a menu at a restaurant. Listing preparation and transaction files in real estate. Quoting and delivery scheduling at a supply yard.

We have looked closely at that stretch in one business so far. The read has to be done business by business, because guessing which pile is the tall one from outside the building is how these projects go wrong.

What this is not

  • Not plant automation, and not robots.
  • Not a website project. That work exists and it is separate.
  • Not a tool purchase, and not a vendor bake-off.
  • Not a replacement for the people doing the work. They are the control on the whole thing.
  • Not nine businesses at once.

03 Why an owner would do this

Four reasons, and they are not the same reason in different clothes. The order is deliberate.

A. The digital work is the cap

A custom shop does not wait on the chopper gun. A firm does not wait on the courtroom. What decides throughput is upstream of the thing that makes the money, and it is made of files. Adding people moves the ceiling one person at a time. Clearing the digital work in front of them is how the same people take on more. Hiring still happens. This does not replace it.

B. Timing

The companies operating at scale are pointing this at digital work first and physical work second. Manufacturing, professional services and hospitality follow that curve on a delay. Being early in a category costs less than catching up to it will. That is a timing argument rather than an outcome, and it is worth naming as such, because it is a reason to act now rather than a result you can measure.

C. The signal

VPC sells to specifying engineers, to OEMs and to government. Tiveron sells to clients choosing a firm. Every one of those buyers is being asked the same AI question inside their own walls right now. A business that can describe a real process, a first draft, a human sign-off and accuracy graded against its own finished work reads as a company worth bringing into a bid. For these customers the story leads with control, not with tools.

D. What you keep even if it fails

This is the one that makes the first step nearly risk free, and it is the one usually left out. Reconstructing finished work and writing down the decision tree puts a business's own operating logic on paper, in most cases for the first time. Right now that logic lives in the heads of the people who quote by hand, intake by instinct and price from memory. If the drafts never get good enough to trust, the business still ends up with a written map of how it actually works and where the time goes. That is worth having on its own terms.

The purpose is a more capable company, not a cheaper one on paper.

04 The process

Six steps, in order, per business. The sequence matters more than the speed.

  1. Sit in the work. Time alongside the people who do it, on live jobs. What arrives, in what form, who chases what, who decides, and what waits on whom. Not a survey and not a questionnaire.
  2. Reconstruct the last ten finished pieces of work in one lane. The inbound artifact as it arrived, the hours, the output, the price, and what changed between the first version and the final one. This is the ground truth for everything after it. If a business cannot assemble those ten, there is no project yet, and we say so rather than build on a guess.
  3. Write the option tree from the business's own material. Its packages, its forms, its actual language. Not a competitor's form and not a generic industry model, because the second one would tell us nothing about this company.
  4. Blind test. Draft against those same ten, without seeing the outcome. The people who did the original work grade the output. The grade is theirs, not ours.
  5. Human sign-off, always. Nothing auto-sends. The output is a draft somebody corrects in minutes, never something a customer sees unattended.
  6. Live work only after the closed-work test holds. One lane, then widen.

Choosing the lane, and one honest complication

Pick on volume and repeat rate, out of what step 1 shows rather than out of whichever example is most interesting to talk about. The complication is that the lane clogging the queue and the lane best suited to a first test are not always the same lane. A backup can come from the most bespoke work in the shop, which is the worst possible place to grade a draft. Where the two diverge, test where the tree is narrow enough to grade, and say plainly that is what was done and why.

05 How anyone would know it worked

Measured against each business's own baselines, which exist inside its own files, and never against a generic industry model. The things worth counting are the same in every one of them: hours of skilled time per first draft, time from a complete request to a first output, hit rate against the finished work being used as the yardstick, rework after the first pass, and work that previously sat waiting.

Every one of those is deliberately blank right now. We do not have the baselines yet, and a number invented here would poison the comparison later.

06 Before any file moves

Each business carries a different constraint on its own records, and the constraint comes before the request rather than after it. VPC's finished jobs are for municipal utilities, industrial plants, the Navy and the Coast Guard, which makes those drawings the customer's business as much as VPC's. A law firm's files carry privilege. The hospitality businesses hold customer and payroll data.

So the rule is the same everywhere. Files stay inside the company's control. Nothing goes into a public model or into anyone's training data. How records are handled gets written down and agreed before the first one moves, and the first document to read is not our policy but the business's own contract and professional obligations, because government agreements and bar rules can dictate how material is stored and who is allowed to see it. If a lane's paperwork cannot clear that, the test moves to a lane whose paperwork can.

07 Where to start

One business, one lane. Not a program across nine.

VPC is the right first one, and the reason is not that it is the largest. It is that the digital work there is unusually well defined, the people who own it are identifiable, and the business has a record of executing on a written document without needing a meeting about it first. Quoting and submittals is the lane. There is a fuller brief on it, and the working session on September 9 is where the questions in step 1 actually get asked.

What we would want from a second business is nothing more than the same first step: time with the people who own the paperwork, and ten finished pieces of work. No software, no commitment, and a real answer inside a couple of weeks about whether there is anything there.
