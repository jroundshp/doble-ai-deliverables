AI across the portfolio

What it means for businesses like these, why an owner would do it, and the process we would follow.

Prepared for Corey Hogan Discussion draft · September 4, 2026 Link-shared, not indexed

The short version

Nine businesses, none of them software companies. What they share is that every job, every matter and every order passes through a stretch of digital work before anybody touches anything physical. That stretch is where each of them backs up, and it is the part AI is genuinely good at today. The plant, the dining room, the courtroom and the yard are people, and they stay people.

So the question is not which tool to buy. It is where the paperwork sits in each business, how much of it repeats, and what a first draft would have to look like for the person who owns that work to trust it. This page is the method. VPC is where it gets tested first.

01 Where this picks up

On August 14 the group met on AI across the portfolio. That morning opened with a concession worth repeating, which is that everybody in the room can already open Claude and the tool was never the constraint.

What has not existed since is a written answer to the obvious next question. This is that document. It is a method rather than a plan, and it is meant to be argued with.

In every business here there is physical work that makes the money, and digital work sitting in front of it deciding how much of the physical work gets done.

What this is not

  • Not plant automation, and not robots.
  • Not a website project. That work exists and it is separate.
  • Not a tool purchase, and not a vendor bake-off.
  • Not a replacement for the people doing the work. They are the control on the whole thing.

03 Why an owner would do this

Four reasons, and they are not the same reason in different clothes.

A. The digital work is the cap

A custom shop does not wait on the chopper gun and a firm does not wait on the courtroom. What decides throughput sits upstream of the thing that makes the money, and it is made of files. Clearing it is how the same people take on more. Hiring still happens.

B. Timing

Companies operating at scale are pointing this at digital work first and physical work second, and manufacturing, professional services and hospitality follow that curve on a delay. Being early costs less than catching up will. Worth naming honestly: that is a reason to act now rather than a result anyone can measure.

C. The signal

VPC sells to specifying engineers, to OEMs and to government. Tiveron sells to clients choosing a firm. Every one of those buyers is being asked the same AI question inside their own walls right now. A business that can describe a real process, a first draft, a human sign-off and accuracy graded against its own finished work reads as one worth bringing into a bid. For these customers the story leads with control, not with tools.

D. What you keep even if it fails

The one usually left out, and the one that makes the first step nearly risk free. Reconstructing finished work and writing down the decision tree puts a business's operating logic on paper, in most cases for the first time. That logic currently lives in the heads of the people who quote by hand, intake by instinct and price from memory. If the drafts never get good enough to trust, the business still ends up with a written map of how it works and where the time goes.

The purpose is a more capable company, not a cheaper one on paper.

04 The process

Six steps, per business, and the sequence matters more than the speed. Two of them are places the whole thing is allowed to stop.

  1. Sit in the work. Time alongside the people who do it, on live jobs. What arrives, in what form, who chases what, and what waits on whom. Not a survey.
  2. Reconstruct the last ten finished pieces of work in one lane: the inbound artifact as it arrived, the hours, the output, the price, and what changed between the first version and the final one. This is the ground truth for everything after it.
  3. Write the option tree from the business's own material. Its packages, its forms, its language. A generic industry model would tell us nothing about this company.
  4. Blind test. Draft against those same ten without seeing the outcome. The people who did the original work grade it. The grade is theirs, not ours.
  5. Human sign-off, always. Nothing auto-sends. The output is a draft somebody corrects in minutes, never something a customer sees unattended.
  6. Live work only after the closed-work test holds. One lane, then widen.

Choosing the lane, and one honest complication

Pick on volume and repeat rate, out of what step 1 shows rather than whichever example is most interesting to talk about. The complication: the lane clogging the queue and the lane best suited to a first test are not always the same lane. A backup can come from the most bespoke work in the shop, which is the worst possible place to grade a draft. Where the two diverge, test where the tree is narrow enough to grade, and say plainly that is what was done and why.

05 How anyone would know it worked

Measured against each business's own baselines, which exist inside its own files, and never against a generic industry model. The things worth counting are the same everywhere: hours of skilled time per first draft, time from a complete request to a first output, hit rate against the finished work used as the yardstick, rework after the first pass, and work that previously sat waiting.

Every one of those is deliberately blank right now. We do not have the baselines, and a number invented here would poison the comparison later.

06 Before any file moves

Each business carries a different constraint on its own records, and the constraint comes before the request rather than after it. VPC's finished jobs are for municipal utilities, industrial plants, the Navy and the Coast Guard, which makes those drawings the customer's business as much as VPC's. A law firm's files carry privilege. The hospitality businesses hold customer and payroll data.

So the rule is the same everywhere. Files stay inside the company's control, nothing goes into a public model or into anyone's training data, and how records are handled gets written down and agreed before the first one moves. The first document to read is not our policy but the business's own contract and professional obligations, because government agreements and bar rules can dictate how material is stored and who may see it. If a lane's paperwork cannot clear that, the test moves to a lane whose paperwork can.

07 Where to start

One business, one lane.

VPC is the right first one, and not because it is the largest. The digital work there is unusually well defined, the people who own it are identifiable, and the business has a record of executing on a written document without needing a meeting about it first. Quoting and submittals is the lane. There is a fuller brief on it, and the working session on September 9 is where the questions in step 1 actually get asked.

What a second business would need is the same first step and nothing more: time with the people who own the paperwork, and ten finished pieces of work. No software, no commitment, and a real answer inside a couple of weeks about whether there is anything there.
