Where this stands now

The twenty-eight questions this brief left open have been answered item by item, and the extraction permit and the seller's acquisition package have now been read in full. Four of the twenty-eight are closed outright, fifteen are partly answered and eight are untouched — the detail is in the review note. This section is the current state of the deal. Everything below it is the underlying detail.

Who answered, and why it is not independent diligence

The responses came from Joel Lee, project estimator at WG Pitts, and they are good: specific, sourced, and right on every figure we were able to re-check against our own copy of the underlying record. But Will Pitts is president of WG Pitts and also president of Structured Global Capital — the entity putting up the $1,000,000 and taking half the deal. Joel sits on the buy side.

That does not make his work wrong, and on operating and island questions — pricing, logistics, plant condition, who the real competitors are — he is the most useful source available. It does mean none of this is third-party diligence, and that the geologist, the quality-of-earnings firm and the title work still have to be engaged by someone with no position in the outcome.

The gating question is not about the quarry

Corey is not named anywhere in the 199 files, and neither is anyone working for him. The $1,000,000 of equity is currently Structured Global Capital's obligation. The operating role is currently KBN's. No teaming agreement between the two buyers exists — it is open item 32 on counsel's own list, and it has not moved.

Everything else on this page is a diligence budget. This is a position, and it should be settled before a dollar of the first is spent, because the answer determines whether Corey is buying into a deal, funding somebody else's, or underwriting the gap between two parties who have not yet agreed terms with each other.

Worth knowing before the next round: the correspondence copies Rhino Ready Mix, John Grealish and Amanda Sargent. "Grealish" appears nowhere in the 199 files, and Rhinoreadymix1@gmail.com appears exactly once, on the KBN non-disclosure agreement's signature page, in a two-column block where the extraction places it ambiguously. The cast on the email is not the cast in the paperwork.

The rock itself has never been tested

  • No borehole has ever been sunk at either site. The 14,191,213.95 m³ Arenales figure is a surveyed boundary volume — the geometry between the permit limits and the depth cap — not a drilled or sampled reserve. Cotto has no reserve document of any kind beyond the seller's letter. There are no boring logs, no core, no geologist's report, nothing on overburden, bedding, clay-filled karst pockets or voids. In karst limestone the distance between boundary volume and recoverable saleable tonnage can be very large, and nothing in this transaction has measured it.
  • The rock has never been qualified for the uses the projections assume, and the one independent comment on it points the wrong way. There is no Los Angeles abrasion result, no sulfate soundness, no ASTM C33 gradation anywhere in the set. The only laboratory result in 199 files is an agricultural-lime assay from June 2023 — CCE 94.6%, genuinely third-party, and silent on construction aggregate. Meanwhile FirstBank's appraiser, describing the competitive set, writes that "Gutierrez polishing grade (required by the US Highway) of its material is excellent for road construction while the fine material or talc type material of Mundo Real is excellent for fine concrete finishes and bonds" DOC. The quarry next door has rock that qualifies for federal-aid highway work; this one is characterised as a fines and concrete-finish material. The asphalt plant and the infrastructure-bidding case are the two largest components of the upside, and both assume a rock nobody has qualified.
  • The recommended answer is a core programme, and it collides with the funding clock. Four holes to 75 feet at each site, eight in total, laid out by a geologist, logged and photographed, with samples out for abrasion, soundness, gradation, absorption and alkali–silica reactivity, producing a report that converts boundary volume into recoverable spec-grade tonnage. A quotation is being sought from Victor Rivera and Associates on the island. Counsel's draft already conditions closing on a geologist's reserve study without defining its scope; this is the scope, and it belongs in §8.1 and Exhibit J with a stated materiality threshold. Cores and laboratory work take several weeks. The letter of intent's undertaking to fund $1,000,000 runs to about 18 September.

The extraction permit resolution, read in full DOC

Case 2017-159059-PCT-011057. Public hearing 7 July 2023, decided by OGPe's Junta Adjudicativa 1 September 2023, resolution signed 27 December 2023, five-year term. Fifty pages of Spanish, and five things in it bear on the price.

1. There is organised, legally recognised community opposition to this quarry. The hearing was contested by the Comité de Aceitunas y Arenales Unidos Pro Ambiente (C.A.U.S.A.) through its president, Reverend Edwin Pérez Román, and by Melanie Colón de Jesús, granted formal intervenor status by resolution. They opposed on dust, traffic and neighbourhood nuisance. The examiner's answer was that the pit sits on the side of the property away from the residential community and that the parties have coexisted "always with a degree of disagreement." This appears nowhere else in the 199 files, in either contract draft, in counsel's 35 items, or in the seller's package. It bears on the asphalt plant, on any below-water-table work and on the next renewal — all contested proceedings, with an organised objector already on the record and a name.

2. The seller let the prior permit lapse. Permit 2017-159059-PCT-001565 was renewable if extension was sought ninety days before expiry — "hecho que no ocurrió." Because the filing was late, OGPe treated it as a new application rather than a renewal, which is what triggered the full public hearing and the intervention. That is what the process looks like next time if it is handled the same way — and the permit runs to 2028 while the seller note runs to about 2031.

3. Extraction below the water table is tightly capped. The 2023 application was itself for extraction bajo el nivel freático, and the governing conditions limit dredging to a pit of no more than 500 square metres of surface, to a maximum of four metres below the water table, with fill maintained, the pit closed before the fill runs out, and a "DANGER — DEEP WATER" sign posted. Separately the permit caps depth at 66 metres above sea level, or one metre above the level of the bordering road. Between them, the 3,000,000 m³ sitting below road level in EMR's own Note D plan — more than half of that plan — is not reachable under anything currently authorised.

4. The permit corroborates the reserve and puts a number on the life of the pit. It sets extraction at 1,700 m³/day, five days a week, fifty-two weeks — 442,000 m³ a year and 2,210,000 m³ across the term — and records that this is 15.57% of the reserve available on the property, which back-solves to about 14,194,000 m³. At that rate it states the deposit would take thirty-two years to work out. Thirty-two years is a real position; the seller's "50+ year reserves" is not what the regulator wrote down.

5. A production history exists, in a government file. As a condition of issuance the examiner required, and the applicant filed, an analysis of the quantity of material actually extracted during the prior permit term, with an updated survey with cross-sections and a cumulative traffic study. No throughput record exists anywhere in the document set. One exists at OGPe, and it is obtainable — the cheapest available answer to how much rock this quarry has actually moved.

The Aguada plant land may not belong to the company being sold

There are two property records at Aguada, not one bad measurement. The Registry parcel — finca 4,490, 10,032 m² — is registered to Hormigonera Chaparro and is what Banco Popular's appraiser valued. The CRIM cadastral record the deal documents use — 068-100-363-14-000, 6.64 acres, the one carrying the structures — lists an owner of record of Héctor Chaparro Carrero, who is not a party to the purchase agreement, with a last sale of $67,100 in October 2004. It is roughly 16,800 m² larger than the titled parcel DOC.

The likeliest explanation is a CRIM grouping error rather than a title defect, and the 2021 appraiser flagged the cadastral mismatch himself at the time. But it cannot be assumed. About 60% of the appraised improvement value at that site is pavement, and if the plant, silos or paving stand on the land that is not finca 4,490, that land belongs to someone else. The title study has to map the improvements onto the finca boundary before anything at that site is priced. The same appraisal places the site in FEMA Zones A and AE, inside the Culebra River path, which makes flood insurance a condition of any financing.

Related and still unexplained: Hormigonera Chaparro's balance sheet carries $3,100,242 of land, identical and undepreciated every year from 2020 to 2024, on a 2.5-acre parcel appraised at $150,482. The likeliest reading is that the financials aggregate more than one property, which points back at the same two parcels.

Utilisation is a schedule problem, not a demand problem

This is the most encouraging finding in the update, because a short week and a worn crushing circuit are fixable in a way that absent demand is not.

The MSHA Form 7000-2 quarterly series, forty quarters, shows about 21 to 22 workers averaging 22 to 27 hours a week against a 72-hour permitted window — Monday to Saturday, 6am to 6pm DOC. 2024 was the highest labour input in thirteen years, 31,467 hours, with quarry revenue flat at $1.6M, and 2025 went higher still at 32,494. The constraint is output per hour and days worked. The appraiser reaches the same place in words: post-María insurance never collected, so repairs instead of replacements, "high operating ratios," "excessive downtime."

Two things to put to the seller: the actual crusher days and shifts, and why 2023 Q2 collapsed to 4,779 hours with headcount down to 18, with no hurricane and no pandemic to explain it.

Covered activity is production and packaging of manufactured sand from crushed stone for industrial use — not quarry extraction, and not either concrete plant DOC. Fifteen years to 31 December 2034 at 4%, with minimums of eight direct employees and $250,000 of annual volume. The summary contains no transfer-of-ownership clause, so the "remains in effect until transfer of ownership" language in circulation is unsourced and the full decree is still needed.   • The MSHA record is good, and it is closing hygiene rather than a price argument. Nineteen years: 111 routine citations and four training withdrawal orders, and no failure-to-abate, unwarrantable-failure, pattern or imminent-danger action of any kind. Zero fatalities across 305,039 operator-hours. Four lost-time injuries in ten years, three in the maintenance workshop. Open and cheap to clear: about $3,500 of unpaid penalties, an October 2025 training withdrawal order, and two citations for the quarterly employment filing itself. Note that the investor safety report in the data room describes a 28-person quarry and calls the injury frequency below the national average; MSHA's own numbers say 21 to 23 people, and on a straight rate basis the frequency is above average — about 2.4 per 200,000 hours against 1.1 to 1.7. The severity profile is genuinely light. Both are true; the report states only one.

Opportunity

The case, and what it rests on

The thesis has three legs: a funded demand cycle, a supply position that is hard to replicate, and a business currently running at a fraction of what its own permit allows.

  • PRIOR Demand is named and funded, not speculative. Western PR has an estimated $7–10B of infrastructure investment identified for 2026–2030 — the $20B LUMA grid rebuild, $4.566B in CDBG-MIT mitigation, the $1.298B City-Rev municipal program, PR-10 expansion at $540–552M, PR-2 freeway conversion, $142M+ at Aguadilla airport and Ramey, and roughly 1 GW of solar. The concrete and aggregates slice is put at $300–500M over five years.
  • PRIOR Supply is genuinely thin. CEMEX is the island's only cement producer. The named western aggregate quarries amount to Productora de Agregados, Cantera Mundo Real, and Rodriguez Aggregates. New quarry permits in karst are slow and contested — so the existing Arenales and Cotto permits are the moat, not the equipment.
  • OURS Is the quarry really running at a fifth of its permit? The permit allows 442,000 m³/yr, ~600,000 tons DOC. At the $14/ton the models assume, full permitted output is roughly $8.4M of aggregate revenue; EMR actually booked $1.6M in 2024 DOC. That arithmetic gives ~20% — but the $14/ton is a model assumption, not a verified market price, and at $10/ton the same sum gives ~27%. A second, independent method agrees. Note D sets out the depletion formula — productive land cost ÷ expected metres × metres produced. Running it backwards from the $42,731 of 2024 depletion gives 89,000–131,000 m³ produced, or 20–30% of the permitted 442,000 m³ DOC, depending on the cost basis. That derivation uses only figures from the accountant's statements and no assumed sale price at all. The question for the group is therefore not whether the gap is real but what is causing it: demand, or trucks, crew and working capital? The upside case rests entirely on that answer.
  • PRIOR An asphalt plant is the swing factor. A 1,700 t/day hot-mix plant sized to match the quarry's permitted output would be fed internally (asphalt is ~95% aggregate by weight). It requires a separate permit that has not been granted, and it is the single largest driver in every projection.

The reserve is contested, and it is the asset case DOC

The seller's package says 14M m³, about 32 years at the permitted rate. EMR's own accountant's notes say something much smaller. Note D of the 2024 financial statements records an external appraiser's estimate of the cubic metres expected to be extracted: 2,200,000 m³ down to the level of the water canal and road, plus 3,000,000 m³ for a further 10 metres below that — 5.2M m³ total, which at 442,000 m³/yr is closer to 12 years than 32.

The 2024 appraisal has now been read, and it largely resolves this. It records that the 2022 permit request shows a reserve of 14,191,213.95 m³, up from 11,776,441.67 m³ in the 2017 permit, and it explains the increase: a named licensed surveyor, Jose O. Quiñones Moret, LS Lic. 17,934, filed a memorandum attributing it to more precise survey instruments and a 10-metre buffer zone where the 2017 filing used a larger one DOC. So the 14M figure is not a sales-deck number — it is a permit-boundary reserve, calculated twice, by a surveyor, with the change between the two filings accounted for.

The two numbers are complementary rather than contradictory: 14.2M m³ is the in-place reserve inside the permit boundary; 5.2M m³ is the constrained two-level extraction plan the company depletes against. The December 2023 permit corroborates the first, recording the term's 2,210,000 m³ as 15.57% of the available reserve and putting the life of the pit at thirty-two years.

The operating question — whether a buyer can get below the water canal and road — is now answered, and the answer is no. The permit caps depth at 66 metres above sea level or one metre above the bordering road, and separately limits any below-water-table dredging to a pit of no more than 500 square metres, four metres deep. The 3,000,000 m³ that sits below road level in EMR's own Note D plan is not reachable under anything currently authorised. And neither figure has ever been tested by a borehole.

Two things the appraisal does not do. It reports the reserve; it does not independently verify it. And it does not use the reserve to derive value at all — the $5.25M is an EBITDA multiple, so none of the appraised value depends on there being 14M m³ rather than 5.2M. An independent reserve study is still the right spend.

A further question falls out of the same note. Cumulative depletion of $795,063 against the appraiser's 5.2M implies roughly a third to a half of that expected reserve has already been extracted, depending on whether the $800,000 of raw and scrap land value is netted out of the $2.5M cost basis first. That does not mean the rock is gone — the Cotto parcel and any deeper extraction sit outside this estimate entirely — but somebody should ask.

The appraisal has now been read, and it prices the deal DOC

This was flagged in the last revision as the most valuable unread document in the set. It is a full USPAP appraisal report, prepared for a bank deciding on its own loan exposure rather than for a sale, and it is the only genuinely independent valuation anyone in this transaction has. It puts the working quarry and the Isabela batch plant together at $5.25M as a going concern — tangible and intangible assets, in aggregate, including $1.5M allocated to the crushers, screens and rolling stock.

Two cautions in both directions. It does not cover Chaparro, and it does not cover the 219-acre Cotto reserve, so $6.1M is not a valuation of this deal — it is the sum of the parts anyone has actually valued. But the arithmetic behind the $5.25M is a projected EBITDA of $750,000 times a multiplier of 7.0, and the projection assumed quarry sales of $2.0M and plant sales of $2.2M. EMR's actual 2024 sales came in at $1,597,692 — 20% under the number the appraisal was built on. The appraiser also notes he valued the same property on 10 November 2022, that the sales increase that appraisal assumed "never materialized," and that the going-concern value therefore fell between the two dates.

The value is also stated under an express extraordinary assumption that the property has all required permits and is free of environmental problems — specifically, that extraction permit renewal case 2023-527771-PCT-PFO-300012 will be granted on similar terms. The Permiso Único the appraiser reviewed, 2022-463171-PU-183184, expired on 11 November 2025. No environmental report was available to him and he recommends a specialist. If those assumptions fail, his own report says the value does not hold.

Decision

What Corey is actually being asked

Two questions, and they are separable.

  • OURS Is the asset good? Probably — but less certainly than the seller's package implies. A permitted limestone quarry in an opportunity zone, in a region with a documented funded pipeline and almost no competing aggregate supply, running at 20–30% of its own permitted output, with a 4% tax decree running to 2034. That is a real position and it is hard to replicate. But the reserve reconciles only on paper, and has never been tested in the ground. The 14.2M m³ is a surveyed permit-boundary volume, corroborated by the regulator's own 15.57% arithmetic and a thirty-two-year life — and no borehole has ever been sunk at either site, and the rock has never been qualified for construction aggregate. The question this brief previously left open — whether a buyer can get below the water canal and road — is answered, and the answer is no: the permit caps depth at the road, and below-water-table work is limited to a 500 m² pit four metres deep. What replaces it is a harder question: how much of that 14.2M m³ is recoverable, saleable, spec-grade stone? Eight core holes and a laboratory suite is the only way anyone finds out.
  • OURS Is this deal good? This is where the answer has moved, and not in the seller's favour. Everything a third party has ever appraised in this group totals $6.1M against a $15M ask — $5.25M for the quarry and Isabela plant from FirstBank's own appraiser in 2024, $850,000 for the Aguada plant from Banco Popular's in 2021. The 219-acre Cotto reserve is unappraised and is the honest bull case for the gap; so is the going-concern value of the businesses Lamadrid did not cover. But the burden has shifted: the seller now has to justify roughly $9M of value that no independent valuer has put a number on, and the appraiser’s own sales comparison sets a “high value limit of $4.5 million” on the quarry going concern. Beyond that, the largest economic term in the transaction, whether $4.5M–$6.5M of bank debt sits inside or on top of the price, has never been agreed, and the interest rate on $14M is still blank.

The gap between those two answers is a diligence budget, not an investment decision. Before committing capital, the sequence counsel lays out is: sign an NDA and get the SSNs out of the data room; form the acquisition entity; agree a term sheet on the price-versus-debt question; then pay for four things — a quality-of-earnings review, title studies on all three parcels, an independent reserve study, and written confirmation from OGPe/DRNA on permit renewal and change of control.

Corey's specific question — whether to put resources here — turns on one thing this document set cannot answer: on which side of the table would he sit? The $1M of equity is currently SGC's obligation, the operating role is currently KBN's, and no teaming agreement between the two buyers exists yet. That is the conversation to have before the diligence conversation.
