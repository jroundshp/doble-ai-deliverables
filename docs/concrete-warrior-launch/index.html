Breanna is standing up the Google Business Profile, and Google asks for a working website during that process. Until this afternoon the answer was a domain that returned an error. The topsoil page is the one worth reading

It is the only page on the site that argues from law rather than from claims, and the two load-bearing lines are quoted straight out of North Dakota Administrative Code 43-02-03. Topsoil is defined there as, in the code's own words, no more than the top eight inches of soil, and reclamation is due within a reasonable time, but not more than one year, after a well is plugged, with the operator's bond sitting behind it. We checked both against the code before the page went up.

That matters commercially because it means the demand does not switch off with the oil price. The page also separates the two certificates buyers use the same word for, which is a genuine source of lost jobs: a weed-free certificate says nothing at all about brine damage, and an oil and gas buyer asking for "certified" topsoil usually means the laboratory analysis.

Seven questions, answered in writing

This is the least obvious item on the page and close to the most valuable. When somebody asks an AI assistant where to buy reclamation topsoil near New Town, the assistant can only repeat what it can read. Of twenty-six producer sites we looked at nationally, two carry anything comparable, and neither of them sells aggregate.

The part worth saying out loud

There is nothing on the live site that cannot be stood behind. Where a number was not confirmed, the section came off rather than going up with a placeholder in it. That is the reason several items further down this document are missing, and it is a deliberate trade: a buyer who finds one invented specification stops believing the rest of the page, and on material going into a structural pour or a reclamation bid that is a compliance problem rather than a marketing one.

Two things that make a batch usable

Send originals. Not screenshots, not images saved out of a text message. Both strip the resolution. An iPhone photo exactly as the phone made it is ideal.

Label the batch on the way in with which yard it is and roughly when it was shot. That context cannot be recovered afterwards, and it is the reason a perfectly good stockpile photograph from an earlier batch still cannot be used: nobody can say what it is a picture of.

One question rather than a request

Is the batch plant selling concrete yet, or is it still running for internal use? We are asking because it changes how the site should present ready-mix, not because anything is wrong. The 4 September production summary shows the yards to date on concrete marked as internal use, while topsoil is running hard and roughly on plan.

If concrete is not commercially batching yet, the fix is small and takes about fifteen minutes: ready-mix stays on the site and keeps its page, moves down the homepage, and reads as a line to call about rather than one with a published delivery radius. Better to ask than to have a buyer ring and order forty yards for a pour before the plant is ready to take it.

What is switched off on purpose

Four decisions, each with the reasoning, each reversible in minutes

These are not gaps waiting to be filled in. They are choices, and if any of them is the wrong call it can be reversed the same day.

Off the site, not off the plan

Buyer prequalification

ISNetworld, Veriforce and Avetta were named on the site along with the fact that none is set up yet. That came off on 3 September. An estimator reads an unanswered prequalification question as a no, and there is no reason to volunteer a disqualifying answer before anybody asks it. Roughly 95% of operators in the basin gate supplier accounts behind one of these, so this carries real commercial weight and is not being dismissed. It goes back on the site the day there is a registration to name.

Showing only what is verified

The credentials list

The list shows the MHA Nation contractor license, which is confirmed. Three others sat beside it reading "Unconfirmed": MSHA Part 46, the weed-free certification and the state approved-source listing. Three rows of "Unconfirmed" tells a buyer we probably do not hold them. Each goes back the moment somebody verifies it, and the weed-free one also unlocks a line on the homepage about source-inspected material.

Coming with the next round

The quote request form

The site takes orders the way the business does: by phone, with quotes back by email, and the four things to have ready are listed beside the number. A form is worth adding and will come. It goes in only once the receiving end exists, because a form that posts nowhere swallows quote requests silently and nobody finds out until a buyer asks why they never heard back.

No longer a gate

The 40-acre lease

This used to be the thing keeping the site hidden from search. It is not any more. The site went live with the lease still unsigned, and the lease gets added to the picture when it is done rather than holding anything up.

The Google listing, and where it has to match

The alignment points, and the three fields that are hard to change afterwards

The full step-by-step is in the Google Business Profile playbook, which has been updated now that the website field has a real answer. This section is only the places where the listing and the website have to agree, because when they disagree it is Google that notices first.

One thing to settle before the profile is created

The listing name and the website currently do not match. The website presents the business as Concrete Warrior. The playbook has been carrying Concrete Warrior Supply as the name to file under, which is also what the logo and the domain say.

Either is defensible. What is not workable is one on the sign, another on the listing and a third on the site. The physical sign should decide it, everything else should follow, and that is one more reason the sign photograph is ranked third in section 02. It is worth two minutes now because a listing name is one of the fields Google makes you fight to change once the profile exists.

Two questions the filing needs answered

Is anybody at the yard during the posted hours?

Google asks this early and it decides what kind of listing this is. If somebody is at 9702 ND-23 through the posted hours, it is a storefront and the address shows publicly. If not, it is a service area business and the address gets hidden. Either is fine and neither is better; guessing is the only bad answer, and it is another field that is awkward to change later.

This is a separate question from whether the phone gets answered. That one is settled, and the number routes to somebody during business hours rather than ringing in an empty yard.

Which account is the profile created from?

Whatever account is used becomes the owner of the listing, and moving a profile afterwards is harder than creating it in the right place. It should be an account the business will still control in two years rather than an individual's personal login.

What happens the day it verifies

Verification is the longest and least predictable wait in this whole project, and almost nothing can be done to the profile until it lands. The moment it does, the second half is quick: categories, the services list, the description, the questions and answers, the photographs and the website link. So the single most useful thing anyone can do is say the day it verifies.

If Google rejects it or the process stalls, that is worth hearing about straight away rather than after a second attempt. What to do next depends entirely on how it failed, and a retry stacked on top of a bad filing is harder to unpick than the original.
