Fall Drives in the Vail Valley: The Three Weeks That Bring the Cars Out

September 20, 2026 · By John Tyler

For about three weeks at the end of every September, this valley fills up with cars that are not from here.

Two of the largest driving events in Colorado finish in Vail in the same week, and the timing is not a scheduling accident. It is the week the aspens turn. The events get the photographs, but they are downstream of the roads, and if you want to understand why Vail sits on the calendar of people who care about cars, the place to start is what is inside an hour of the Main Vail roundabout. This is the best three weeks of the year to be driving here, and the car world worked that out a long time before I did.

Why the end of September

Color here works by elevation, not by date. It starts high and comes down, so the passes above 10,600 feet turn while the cottonwoods on the valley floor are still green, and you can drive through three weeks of autumn in ninety minutes if you pick the right road. That vertical spread is the whole trick, and it is why a drive beats a single overlook.

I will be straight about this year. After the warmest winter in memory, a record-low snowpack, and a summer that put Eagle County in the worst drought category on the map, the foresters and color trackers the Summit Daily talked to expect 2026 to peak seven to ten days early and to look less vibrant than most years, with some stands going brown instead of gold and dropping fast once they turn. Drought-stressed aspens do that. If you have been holding out for a perfect week, this is not the year for it. Go now, and go high first. The same dry summer I wrote about in the piece on water rights and the call on the Eagle River is the reason the leaves are early.

Five drives

Shrine Pass. If you take one, take this one. About eleven miles of dirt from the Vail Pass summit down into Red Cliff, topping out near 11,000 feet, with aspen on the lower slopes and the Mount of the Holy Cross off to the southwest. It is downhill nearly the whole way from the Vail Pass side, which makes the rough sections easier. A normal car handles it in dry weather. A lowered car will not enjoy the last mile into Red Cliff. Allow two hours so you can stop.

Top of the Rockies, from Minturn. US 24 south out of Minturn climbs to Tennessee Pass at 10,424 feet and drops into Leadville. It is pavement the whole way, it crosses the Continental Divide, and the byway it belongs to almost never falls below 9,000 feet across its 82-mile run to Copper Mountain. At the Tennessee Pass summit there is a marker on the east side of the road for the 10th Mountain Division, which I have written about before. Camp Hale is on the way up. Half a day, comfortably.

Red Sandstone Road to Piney Lake. North out of Vail on dirt, roughly an hour and a half each way with stops, and the payoff is a lake with the Gore Range standing behind it and aspen on both sides of the road most of the way up. This is the one locals do, and it is the one that gets crowded on a blue Saturday in early October. Go on a weekday.

Independence Pass. The long one, and the one with a deadline. Colorado 82 tops out at 12,095 feet between Twin Lakes and Aspen, and CDOT typically closes it on or near November 7, reopening the Thursday before Memorial Day. Nothing over 35 feet is allowed on it at any time, vehicle and trailer measured together, so leave the boat at home. Run it as a loop from Vail by way of Leadville and come home over the top, and you have a full day and one of the better roads in North America.

Vail Pass and Loveland Pass, for an evening. When you only have ninety minutes, go east on I-70 to Frisco and come back, or keep going and take US 6 over Loveland Pass at nearly 12,000 feet. Both are high enough to show color before anything down here does.

What comes through while the color is up

The Colorado Grand is the one most people in Vail have seen without knowing what they were looking at. Roughly 85 sports and racing cars, all built in 1960 or earlier, covering about 1,000 miles of Colorado in five days, starting and ending here. Bob Sutherland started it in 1989. It is not a race and never has been, the daily route is deliberately never published so the passes do not clog, and it runs in cooperation with the State Patrol. It has given away more than $11 million since 1989, better than $800,000 a year now, to the State Patrol Family Foundation, Flight for Life, CASA, clinics in towns most people drive past, and college scholarships. The 2026 tour ran September 14 through 19.

The Vail Automotive Classic has been here since 2009, and this year was the seventeenth. It is three days, not one: a curated road tour on Friday with a catered lunch and groups split by how hard people want to drive, coffee and cars on the lawn at the gondola base on Saturday morning, and the concours itself filling Lionshead on Sunday across twelve classes. Spectators pay nothing. It is run by a 501(c)(3) that puts the proceeds back into local organizations, the Vail Valley Charitable Fund and the Vail Resorts EpicPromise Employee Foundation among them. One board member, Ron Kirkham, once described the whole thing to the Vail Daily in a sentence I have never been able to improve on: “We are all car nuts in one form or another.”

It did not start in Lionshead. In 2010 it was called Wheels and Wings and ran at the Vail Valley Jet Center, 130 cars parked alongside about thirty airplanes, with close to 3,000 people through on the Saturday. Best of Show that year went to a 1950 Riley from Denver.

None of that is the whole picture, either. Clubs run their own tours through here on the same three-week window, and the Rocky Mountain region of the Ferrari Club has a standing fall foliage run from Denver over Independence Pass that pulls forty cars in a good year. And the downvalley version of all of this is free and has nothing to do with concours judging: the Gypsum Creek Cruisers show up every July at Gypsum Daze with street rods, muscle cars, pickups, and whatever somebody finished over the winter.

The altitude tax

A naturally aspirated engine loses roughly three percent of its power for every 1,000 feet it climbs. Drive a 300-horsepower car from sea level up to Tennessee Pass and you arrive making something nearer 210. Visitors feel it on the first climb out of town and almost always blame the car.

Which is what makes the Colorado Grand harder than it looks from the sidewalk. Those are carbureted cars from before 1961, jetted for air that is not there, crossing the Divide repeatedly, on original brakes, with no cell coverage for long stretches. It looks like a parade. It is closer to an endurance run. Nobody is setting a time up here, and that is rather the point of the place.

Where the car actually lives

This is where my job starts. People fall in love with the driving season and then discover that owning a car in a ski town is its own problem, and it is a problem best solved before you buy rather than after.

Vail clears its streets in winter by not letting you park on them. From the start of snow removal season in mid-November until late April there is no on-street or shoulder parking anywhere in town, and it is not an overnight rule. It applies at any hour, it covers construction vehicles, and a car left out can be ticketed or towed. That one rule drives a surprising amount of real estate decision-making up here. Most Vail condominiums convey exactly one deeded space, frequently unheated, sometimes tandem, and adding a second one ranges from expensive to impossible. If you are bringing more than one car, or a car you care about, the garage is not a detail on the listing. It is the constraint.

The things that matter, in the order they come up: whether the garage is heated, the ceiling height if you ever want a lift, whether there is a floor drain, and what the association actually permits inside a garage, since some prohibit lifts, trailers, and anything that looks like ongoing work. One more that catches people out: the driveway is yours to clear, and anyone you hire to plow it needs a snowplow license from Town of Vail Public Works, so you cannot simply find a truck in December. Down the valley the math changes. Homes in Eagle and Gypsum come with real garages as a matter of course, and the commercial collector storage has concentrated out near the county airport, which is no accident: people fly in, pull a car out, and drive it up the valley.

So if a garage is on your list, tell me on the first call rather than the tenth showing. It narrows a search faster than bedroom count does, and of everything a buyer wants in a mountain home, covered space for cars is the single hardest thing to add later.

Frequently Asked Questions

When do fall colors peak in the Vail Valley?

Usually the last two weeks of September into the first week of October, and it moves down the mountain rather than arriving all at once. The high passes above 10,600 feet turn first and the valley floor is last. In 2026, after a record-low snowpack and a drought summer, forecasters expect peak to run seven to ten days early and to look duller than a normal year, with leaves dropping faster once they turn.

What is the best fall drive near Vail?

Shrine Pass, if your schedule allows one drive. It runs roughly 11 miles of dirt road from the Vail Pass summit down to Red Cliff, topping out near 11,000 feet, through aspen groves with the Mount of the Holy Cross in view. It is passable in a normal car in dry conditions and rougher at both ends. For a paved alternative, take US 24 from Minturn over Tennessee Pass on the Top of the Rockies byway.

When does Independence Pass close for the winter?

The Colorado Department of Transportation says Colorado Highway 82 over Independence Pass typically closes on or near November 7 and almost always reopens the Thursday before Memorial Day. The summit is 12,095 feet. No vehicle over 35 feet may use the pass at any time, and that includes a vehicle and trailer measured together.

What is the Colorado Grand?

A charity driving tour founded in 1989 by Bob Sutherland that starts and ends in Vail. About 85 sports and racing cars built in 1960 or earlier cover roughly 1,000 miles of Colorado in five days, timed to fall color. It is not a race. The daily route is deliberately not published, to keep traffic off the passes. The event raises well over $800,000 a year and has given more than $11 million since 1989 to Colorado charities including the Colorado State Patrol Family Foundation, Flight for Life, CASA, small-town medical clinics, and scholarships for graduating seniors. The 2026 tour ran September 14 through 19.

Is the Vail Concours free to watch?

Yes. Spectators are welcome on foot at no charge and donations are encouraged. The Vail Automotive Classic has run since 2009 and is put on by the Vail Automotive Classic Foundation, a 501(c)(3) whose proceeds go to local nonprofits, including the Vail Valley Charitable Fund and the Vail Resorts EpicPromise Employee Foundation. The 2026 event ran September 18 through 20, with the concours itself filling Lionshead on Sunday from 9 a.m. to 3 p.m. across twelve classes.

Where do you keep a car in Vail through the winter?

Indoors, or downvalley. From the start of snow removal season in mid-November until late April, the Town of Vail allows no on-street or shoulder parking anywhere in town at any hour of the day, construction vehicles included, and a car left out can be ticketed or towed. Clearing your own driveway, parking area, and sidewalk is the owner's job, and a contractor you hire to plow needs a snowplow license from Town of Vail Public Works. Most Vail condominiums convey one deeded space, often unheated and sometimes tandem, which is why garage square footage is one of the first things collectors ask me about. Long-term and collector storage has clustered downvalley near the Eagle County airport in Gypsum.
