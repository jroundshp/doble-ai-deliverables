07

When the card is final, what the website has to do with it

A printed card and a machine-readable one are two different deliverables. The second one is where the searchability lives, and it is the step that usually gets skipped. What follows is the state of the site as fetched on 2 September 2026, then what a new card needs from it.

What the site does well right now

  • The menu is real HTML text. Every item name sits next to its price and its description in the page source. That is the single most valuable thing on the site and it is the only reason the comparison in this readout was possible at all. A search engine reads it, and so does an assistant answering a question about dinner in Cooperstown.
  • The menu page carries a correct canonical tag.
  • The address and the 11AM to 9PM hours are both present in the page text.

What a crawler and an assistant cannot see

  • There is no structured data anywhere. Zero JSON-LD blocks on the homepage and zero on the menu page. No Restaurant, no Menu, no MenuItem, no address, hours, phone or geo in markup, even though the address and hours are sitting in the text.
  • The dinner section is not a heading. “Entrees after 5pm” is plain body text. Starters, Salad, Sandwiches, Burgers, Flatbread Pizza, Kids Menu and Dessert are h3. Soup, Weekly Specials and Create Your Own Pasta are h2. The one section this entire readout is about is the one section a machine cannot see as a section.
  • robots.txt points at a sitemap that 404s. It declares Sitemap: https://www.fenimorestable.com/wp-sitemap.xml, and that URL returns 404, as do /sitemap.xml and /sitemap_index.xml.
  • No meta description on the homepage or the menu page.
  • No Open Graph tags, so the link renders without a title card when anyone shares it in a text message or on Facebook.
  • No llms.txt.

What the new card needs, in order

  1. Keep it as textWhatever the new card looks like on paper, the web version stays HTML text with the price beside the name. A PDF of the printed card, or an image of it, would delete the one asset the site currently has. This is the item most likely to go wrong, because a finished design file is the easy thing to upload.
  2. Give the dinner section a real heading“Entrees after 5pm” becomes a heading at the same level as Starters and Sandwiches, and the new groups on the card, To Share, Soups and Greens, Bowls, Supper, From the Oven, Dessert, each get one too. Peer sections at one level. This is a Divi setting, not a rebuild.
  3. Mark up the restaurant, then the menuRestaurant on the homepage carrying the address, phone, the 11AM to 9PM hours, geo coordinates, the Toast reservation link as acceptsReservations, and hasMenu pointing at the menu page. Then Menu with a MenuSection per group and a MenuItem per dish, each with its name, description and price. This is what turns “where can I get schnitzel near Cooperstown” into a question the site can answer.
  4. The markup has to match the printed card exactlyIf the two disagree, the schema gets cut down to what is actually on the page, never the other way round. That means the naming has to be settled first: Moody Blue Chips or Blue Cheese Chips, Hot Pepperoni or Hot Honey Pepperoni, one price for the banana bread. Weekly specials either get written into the page each week or the markup leaves them out. A MenuItem for a dish nobody can order is worse than no markup at all.
  5. Fix the declared sitemap, add meta descriptions and Open GraphThree small items that have nothing to do with the menu and are worth doing while somebody is already in the site. The sitemap in robots.txt has to resolve. The menu page needs a description written for a person, not a keyword. Open Graph tags mean the link looks like a restaurant when it gets shared.
  6. Then an llms.txt, and not beforeA short plain-text file that tells an assistant what the restaurant is, where it is, when it is open and what is on the card. It is only worth publishing once the menu page underneath it is stable and the schema matches, otherwise it just states the confusion more confidently. Do items 01 through 05, then this takes twenty minutes.

Why this is worth more than usual here Section 03 found five formats that did not appear on any Cooperstown card we could read: schnitzel, plated pot roast, katsu and Japanese curry, paprikash, pierogi. Four of them are on the proposed card. A dish with no local competitor is exactly the dish an assistant has no other answer for, and the only way it gets picked up is if the item name, the description and the price are readable at the item level. Adding the dishes and leaving the site as it is would put four uncontested items behind a page that a machine reads as one undifferentiated block of text.
