The #203 all-in figure is now the strongest number we have

An actual buyer paid $3,200,000 for an original-condition unit in this building and is spending $800,000 to $1,000,000 to finish it. That puts their all-in cost for a renovated Edelweiss two-bedroom at roughly $4.0 to $4.2 million, or about $3,676 to $3,860 per square foot.

Two honest caveats. A renovation budget is a cost, not a market price, and work done to one owner's taste does not return dollar for dollar. And #203's square footage (~1,088) is from the research pack rather than verified. Even discounted for both, this is the closest like-for-like evidence in existence, it is in the subject's own building, and it brackets our recommended band almost exactly.

#302 changed the argument in July. The earlier draft of this brief treated #302 as an active twin ask that killed the scarcity story. It came off the market in July 2026 without ever reducing from $4,950,000, so that argument is retired. What replaces it cuts two ways and both are worth saying out loud:

  • For the owner. #301 is now the only Edelweiss unit a buyer can purchase, and with #203 a construction site for about a year, the only finished one. That is genuine scarcity with a shelf life of roughly twelve months.
  • Against the price. Two units in this building asked $4,950,000. One withdrew rather than test a lower number. Neither sold. That is two independent sellers failing at the same price point.

How the two numbers relate

$4,500,000 is a negotiating position. $4,250,000 is a clearing price. Nothing that closed in Vail Village in 2026 supports $4,029 per square foot for a two-bedroom, so a $4,500,000 ask will bring buyers back to the table and then be worked. At the 90.8 percent Village Center 3G actually took, $4,500,000 lands near $4,086,000, which is inside our band. The two numbers are not in conflict; they are the same outcome reached by different routes.

The route matters because of the timeline. The owner wants a buyer this season. A $4,500,000 ask that needs sixty days of negotiation to reach $4.1 million is slower than a $4,300,000 ask that draws an offer in three weeks. If the season is the constraint, the lower band is the faster instrument.

Why the $4.15M to $4.35M band

  • It matches what a real buyer is spending in this building. The #203 owner's all-in cost for a finished unit works out to roughly $3,676 to $3,860 per square foot. Our band is $3,715 to $3,894. That is as close to a like-for-like as this market produces.
  • It stays well above #203's purchase price. At $4,250,000 the unit holds a $1,050,000 premium over the original-condition sale in its own building, which is the renovation, the corner, the two deeded spaces and the sauna paid for in full.
  • It sits above the Village Center 3G close, not below it. $4,250,000 against a $3,950,000 close for nearly identical square footage keeps a defensible Edelweiss and parking premium rather than conceding the comparison.
  • It moves out of the new-construction fight. At $4,250,000 the buyer comparing against Altus at $4,895,000 is looking at a $645,000 saving, which reframes the square-footage difference.
  • It leaves negotiating room that matches the market. If a buyer works the same 90.8 percent, a $4,250,000 ask still lands near $3,860,000, inside the band where Village two-bedrooms are closing.

Confidence, stated plainly

Strong that a material reduction is needed. Two seasons of marketing, forty-five open houses, a $350,000 cut that produced nothing, a second unit in the building that withdrew at the same price, and a $1 million gap to the closest comparable sale all point the same way.

Stronger than the last draft on the floor. The special-assessment risk is now closed, and the #203 renovation gives us a like-for-like anchor we did not have on September 5.

Medium on the exact dollar. The sold set behind the $3.4 million to $4.0 million band is still not fully enumerated, #203's square footage and renovation budget are reported rather than verified, and the three-to-five-bedroom sales that JT says are driving the high per-foot numbers are not in our data.

One structural point for the owner conversation

A single decisive cut reads very differently to the market than a third small step. The property has already made one $350,000 reduction that did not produce a buyer. Another $200,000 move would most likely produce the same result and would spend the remaining credibility of the price.

The owner's stated floor was about $4.7 million last spring. Both numbers on this page are below it, so the conversation is the same conversation either way: the spring expectation is no longer supported, and the evidence for that is now in their own building rather than across town.
