What this actually is Doc

A Request for Information is market research. An agency uses it to find out what industry can do before it decides whether to buy anything, and a response creates no rights on either side. There is no award in this packet, no solicitation number beyond the RFI reference, no contract number and no period of performance.

TDS offers 2,000 berthing units and 500 MWR units across ten installations, from Great Lakes to Pearl Harbor, on a turnkey lease. The cover letter is direct about the structure: “Although we can sell all the equipment, we do not see any advantage to the Government in purchasing these items.”

Open The files show a letter dated 23 August 2026 and a completed solution tab. Nothing in them confirms that anything was submitted or received.

Why this one matters more than the others

A capability package prepared for a federal contracting office states a $300 million contract value for an award that, on its face, guarantees nothing. Whatever the explanation, this is the entry to ask about first, and it is the only one in the whole history we can independently test.

Open There may be an innocent reading. It could be a program ceiling somebody quoted verbally, an aggregate of the state’s whole Helene housing spend, or a placeholder nobody revisited. None of those readings appear in the document. The line immediately above it in the same list reads $24,755,00.00, which is missing a digit, so the list is not being proofread.

Two attempts to knock this finding down both failed, which is why it leads the page. The figure is not in the solicitation. The only dollar amounts anywhere in the 32-page North Carolina IFB are a $1,000,000 insurance threshold and two small-purchase figures in the attachments; nothing in the document TDS bid against could be the source of $300 million. And it is not a different contract. The same history lists four other North Carolina engagements, all of them from Hurricane Floyd in 1999 or the 2008 and 2011 pre-positioning terms. Entry 49 is the only one from 2025, it names Helene, and its dates land within a day of the award letter at both ends.

Open One door stays open and this set cannot close it: if TDS holds some separate North Carolina turnkey agreement that appears in none of these documents, the entry could be describing that instead. Asking is cheaper than assuming either way.

Ours This also reaches backwards. Our North Carolina brief leans on two entries from this same contract history as the best available evidence of what that award is worth. Those two survived a cross-check, but the list they sit in now contains one entry we can prove overstates a contract we hold. That lowers the weight the history can carry, and the North Carolina page has been updated to say so.

Open The file is a downloaded copy. Whether a completed version was filed separately is not something these files can answer, and it is worth confirming before anyone treats it as a defect.

A lessor’s form contract, bundled into market research Doc

Pages 29 to 32 are Rental Schedule No. 26281, the standard terms of Government Leasing, LLC, whose owner Tom Wittwer is described in the packet as having founded the firm in 1981 and as the largest independent lessor in the federal market. Vendor

The schedule opens with an instruction rather than an offer: to accept the pricing, a specified sentence “must appear in the Schedule of Supplies and Services of the Delivery Order issued by the Government.” Several of its terms are worth a lawyer’s eye before this travels any further:

  • Title stays with the lessor. “The Government shall have no right or interest in the Equipment,” held subordinate to the contractor and its assignee.
  • The Government carries risk of loss from the commencement date, and must replace lost or damaged equipment with no abatement of the lease payments.
  • Renewal is presented as a commitment. The Government “commits to renew the rental each fiscal year,” must not reprogram funds available to pay it, and must not replace the equipment with anything functionally similar during the term. Non-appropriation is the only stated escape.
  • The term does not agree with itself. The period of performance is stated as 60 months, and the table beneath it runs a base period plus three renewals, ending at month 48.
  • A 4% handling fee applies to any payment made by government purchase or credit card.

Ours Attaching a financier’s form terms to an RFI is not itself improper. But it turns a capability response into something that reads like a proposed deal, and every one of the clauses above is the sort a contracting officer strikes. Corey’s exposure is reputational rather than contractual at this stage, since none of it binds anyone.

Nothing is built yet Doc

Units currently available: zero. All 2,500 sit in the workbook’s to-be-developed column. Production is claimed through Forest River for berthing units at up to 250 per day, with CAVCO Industries building the MWR units, and a stated ramp of 50 units per day within ten calendar days rising to 300 per day within thirty. Vendor

Two capacity fields do not reconcile, and the way they fail is the interesting part. Total Units is a live formula, units available plus units to be built, and it returns 2,500. Total Beds is a formula field too, but the formula has been deleted and 11,900 typed in its place. At the six beds per unit the workbook states, 2,500 units give 15,000 beds. And 11,900 happens to be the figure the cover letter uses for the Navy’s own headcount: “approximately 11,900 United States Navy personnel.”

Ours The likeliest reading is that the requirement was entered where the arithmetic belonged. The packet does not say so, which leaves the bed count this response actually offers unresolved: 15,000 if the units are real, 11,900 if the beds are. Surge capacity is a separate field, answered 500.

The same tab reports Development Status as “Operating.” Nothing is operating. That tab is also where units currently available is answered zero.

The Hawaii installations would be served by ocean barge from Naval Station San Diego to Pearl Harbor.

Ours This is the same shape as the North Carolina exposure. The capability is real on paper and the fleet does not exist, so everything depends on a manufacturer relationship that no document in this set evidences with a contract.

The packet was not written for this customer Vendor

The catering submission, which carries the only prices in the packet, is addressed to “Make Farrall” and refers to the people being fed as soldiers eleven times in a document going to the Navy. Its author is MCM Solutions, LLC, while the TDS cover letter names the food partner as Morgan Chase Management. Both names appear across the packet without either being reconciled to the other.

The president’s email address is printed with a comma in place of the period, exactly as it was in the North Carolina package twenty months earlier. Company tenure is given as “over 32 years” here and “over 28 years” in the North Carolina package, describing the same company.

Ours On its own this is cosmetic. Set against the $300 million entry and the empty workbook tabs, it points at the same underlying thing: these packages are assembled at speed from earlier ones and nobody reads them cold before they go out.

What this brief has not seen Open

  • The RFI itself. We have TDS’s answer, not the Navy’s question. Requirements, evaluation approach and timing all sit in a document nobody here has read.
  • Any proof of submission. No receipt, no portal confirmation, no acknowledgement.
  • Four pages of the packet. Pages 25 to 28 are images with no extractable text, and are almost certainly floorplans.
  • Any agreement with Forest River, CAVCO, Government Leasing, MCM or Morgan Chase. Every capability in the offer belongs to somebody else, and nothing here binds any of them.
  • Corey’s position. Nothing in these files describes his stake, the Phoenix teaming arrangement, or how any of it would be papered.

Three questions, in order

  1. Where does the $300,000,000 North Carolina figure come from?

    Ask before anything else, and ask TDS directly. It is in a package prepared for a federal agency, and the award letter for that contract sets no value at all. There may be a clean answer. Until there is one, every other number TDS supplies needs its own check.

  2. What does a berthing unit cost per month?

    The packet answers this nowhere. Without it there is no way to test whether the Navy opportunity is worth anything to Corey, and no way to compare it against the other four in the portfolio.

  3. Was a completed workbook actually filed, with the company and location tabs populated?

    If the version we have is what went in, the response may not survive an initial screen regardless of its merits. If a fuller copy exists, we should be reading that one instead.
