Holding Title in an LLC or a Trust: What It Does for a Vail Valley Owner

September 6, 2026 · By John Tyler

Over the years I have had this conversation with a lot of owners, and it usually starts the same way. Somebody on the gondola mentioned that they hold their place in an LLC, or that they set up a trust in Wyoming, and the question arrives already half formed: should I be doing that too?

It is a fair question and the honest answer is that it depends on things I am not qualified to rule on. I am not an attorney and I am not an accountant, and how you hold title is squarely their work rather than mine. What I can do is lay out what these structures actually are, what they tend to accomplish for owners here, and which parts of the popular version do not survive contact with Colorado. That is usually enough for an owner to walk into a conversation with their advisors knowing what to ask.

One thing worth saying at the start, because it reframes the rest: the reasons that hold up in this valley are liability and estate planning. The reasons that come up most often in conversation, saving state tax and staying anonymous, are the two that hold up least well here.

The three structures people usually mean

Almost every version of this question is really about one of three things, and they solve different problems.

A revocable living trust is an estate planning instrument. You put the property into it, you keep control, and you can undo it. Its main effect is that when you die the property passes under the trust rather than through probate. It does not separate the asset from you for liability purposes, because you never really let go of it.

An LLC is a liability instrument. It places a legal entity between the property and the rest of what you own. It is a real business with its own filings and its own bank account, and it does nothing for your estate plan on its own.

An irrevocable trust is a different animal entirely, because it involves genuinely giving up control of the asset. That is a significant decision with consequences well beyond real estate, and it belongs with your attorney rather than in a blog post.

Owners who want both the liability effect and the estate effect sometimes end up with both, the trust holding the LLC interest rather than the property directly. That is common enough to be unremarkable and complicated enough that nobody should assemble it from reading.

What it does about liability

This is the strongest of the reasons, and it is also the one most often oversold. An entity can limit which of your assets are exposed if something happens at the property. It does not make you untouchable, and it only works if the entity is genuinely maintained as one: its own account, its own records, no mixing of personal and entity money. Where owners run into trouble is treating an LLC as a name on a deed rather than as a business that has to be kept.

It also does not replace insurance, and the owners for whom this matters most, the ones renting a property out, are generally carrying liability coverage written for that use no matter how title is held. If you are weighing this alongside a rental, the short-term rental rules are a separate gate and the HOA usually decides more than the entity does.

What it does about privacy, and what changed this year

The anonymity argument is the one that has moved most, and anything written on this subject before this spring is describing a different landscape.

FinCEN's Residential Real Estate Rule was written to require reporting on non-financed residential transfers into entities and trusts, which is precisely the transaction people were using for privacy. On March 19, 2026 the U.S. District Court for the Eastern District of Texas vacated that rule. FinCEN, working with the Department of Justice, has appealed. FinCEN's own guidance issued May 18, 2026 states that reporting persons are not currently required to file Real Estate Reports and are not subject to liability if they fail to do so while the court's order remains in force.

So as of this writing the reporting requirement is not in effect, and it may come back. The practical read is not that privacy is gone or that it is safe, but that it is unsettled, and building a structure primarily around a privacy benefit means building it on something that has moved twice inside a year. Anyone whose reasoning rests on that point should be asking their attorney where the appeal stands rather than relying on this or any other article.

What it does about taxes here, which is less than people expect

Colorado assesses property tax on the property rather than on its owner, so the entity holding title does not change the bill. There is no Colorado state real estate transfer tax, and no franchise tax on LLCs. The state documentary fee is one cent per one hundred dollars of consideration, which on any valley property rounds to a rounding error.

That is genuinely good news, and it also removes most of the tax argument. There is not much state tax to save here, which means a structure sold to you primarily as a Colorado tax strategy is being sold on a benefit that mostly does not exist. What Colorado does offer is cost and simplicity: forming an entity here is inexpensive and the annual periodic report fee is twenty five dollars.

On the Wyoming comparison, since it comes up every time: Wyoming is marketed on anonymity and creditor protection, and Colorado does not compete on those terms. The more useful point is that Colorado real property is governed by Colorado law and recorded in a Colorado county regardless of where an entity was formed. Forming elsewhere does not move the property, and an out-of-state entity holding property here generally has to register in Colorado anyway, which brings back the registered agent and the annual filing.

The local cost nobody warns you about

Here is the part that is specific to this valley and absent from every national article on the subject.

Moving a property into an entity is itself a conveyance, and several jurisdictions here tax conveyances at rates that make that transfer expensive. The Town of Vail imposes a 1 percent real estate transfer tax. The Town of Avon imposes 2 percent. The Beaver Creek Resort Company imposes a transfer assessment of 2.375 percent of fair market value. Those are current as of publication and are an indication of scale rather than a quote for any particular address.

Whether a specific transfer into a trust or an LLC is exempt from those is not something to assume in either direction. Vail's municipal code contains a list of exemptions and, separately, a section governing how an exemption is applied for, which tells you that an exemption there is claimed rather than granted automatically. Colorado's state documentary fee works the same way at the county level, where an exemption has to be claimed when the deed is presented rather than applied by the clerk.

The principle worth carrying out of this section is the one that governs most local money questions here: every town and resort community sets its own rules, so two properties twenty minutes apart can close very differently. The title company handling your transaction is the right authority for your specific address, and that question is worth asking before signing rather than after recording. The same principle shows up in the 1031 exchange guide, where these local transfer taxes are among the things an exchange does not defer.

The mortgage question

If there is a loan on the property, this deserves its own conversation before anything is recorded.

Federal law under the Garn-St Germain Act restricts a lender from calling a loan due when a residence is transferred into a revocable trust in which the borrower is a beneficiary. That protection is built around trusts. It does not extend in the same way to a transfer into an LLC, which means moving a mortgaged property into one can put you in front of a due-on-sale clause.

In practice lenders frequently consent when they are asked. The difference that matters is the sequence. Asking a lender for consent is an ordinary conversation. Recording first and explaining afterward is a different one.

Where it tends to fit

Across the owners I have worked with, the situations where this comes up and holds up share a few features.

  • Owning in more than one state, where probate in each is the thing being avoided.
  • Owning with partners, siblings, or several family branches, where the operating agreement is doing real work.
  • Renting the property out, where the liability separation has something concrete to separate.
  • An estate plan already underway, where the property is one asset among several being organized.
  • A property intended to pass to children, where the goal is avoiding a probate process in a state they do not live in.

And the situations where owners look at it and generally decide against:

  • A single primary residence with a mortgage and no rental use, where the cost and the lender conversation outweigh the benefit.
  • A purchase where the transfer tax on moving it into the entity is a real number and the benefit is speculative.
  • A structure being adopted mainly for anonymity, which is the reasoning that has aged worst this year.
  • An owner who will not maintain the entity properly, since an LLC kept badly offers less than it appears to.

What it costs you

The direct costs are modest. Formation is inexpensive here and the annual report is twenty five dollars. The costs owners underestimate are the ongoing ones: a separate account and the bookkeeping that goes with it, a registered agent if you are not local, additional accounting work, the lender conversation, and potentially a transfer tax on the transfer itself.

None of that is a reason not to do it. It is a reason to know the number before deciding, because the benefit is often real and the cost is often larger than the version you heard on the lift suggested.

Who belongs in this conversation

Your attorney and your CPA, and on the transfer tax question, the title company handling the transaction. I am neither an attorney nor an accountant, and nothing here is tax or legal advice. What I can tell you is what I see owners here run into, which parts of the standard advice do not fit Colorado, and which questions tend to get skipped until they are expensive.

If you are weighing this on a property you own or one you are considering, I am glad to talk it through alongside your advisors and to get the transfer tax question answered by the title company before it becomes a surprise. Frequently Asked Questions

Should I buy Vail Valley property in an LLC or a trust?

That depends entirely on your own situation, and it is a question for your attorney and your CPA rather than a rule of thumb. What is generally true is that the reasons that hold up in Colorado are liability separation and estate planning, and the reasons that tend not to hold up are state tax savings and anonymity. Owners who hold in more than one state, own with partners, rent the property out, or want to keep the property out of probate are the ones for whom it comes up most often.

Does an LLC or trust save you money on Colorado property taxes?

No. Colorado assesses property tax on the property, not on who owns it, so the entity holding title does not change the bill. Colorado also has no state real estate transfer tax and no franchise tax on LLCs, which means there is little state tax to save in the first place. Anyone selling an entity structure primarily as a Colorado tax strategy is describing a benefit that mostly does not exist here.

Does transferring my Vail property into a trust or an LLC trigger the real estate transfer tax?

It depends on the jurisdiction and on the specific transfer, and it is worth answering before you sign rather than after. The Town of Vail imposes a 1 percent real estate transfer tax, the Town of Avon 2 percent, and the Beaver Creek Resort Company a 2.375 percent transfer assessment. Vail's code contains a list of exemptions and a separate section covering how an exemption is applied for, so an exemption there is claimed rather than automatic. Whether a particular transfer into an entity qualifies is a question for the title company handling it and for your attorney, and the answer can differ between two properties twenty minutes apart.

Is buying property in an LLC still private in 2026?

Less so than it was, and the picture is genuinely unsettled. FinCEN's Residential Real Estate Rule was written to require reporting on non-financed residential transfers to entities and trusts. On March 19, 2026 the U.S. District Court for the Eastern District of Texas vacated that rule, and FinCEN has appealed. FinCEN's own guidance issued May 18, 2026 states that reporting persons are not currently required to file Real Estate Reports and are not subject to liability if they fail to do so while the court's order remains in force. That could change if the appeal succeeds, so anyone relying on privacy as the main reason for a structure is relying on something that has moved twice in a year.

What is the difference between a revocable trust and an LLC for holding real estate?

They solve different problems. A revocable living trust is primarily an estate planning tool: you keep control, you can undo it, it does not separate the asset from you for liability purposes, and its main effect is that the property passes without probate. An LLC is primarily a liability tool: it puts a legal entity between the property and your other assets, it is a separate filer with its own paperwork, and it does not by itself do anything for your estate plan. Owners who want both effects sometimes use both, with the trust holding the LLC interest.

Will moving my property into an LLC trigger the due-on-sale clause on my mortgage?

It can, and this is the part owners most often miss. Federal law under the Garn-St Germain Act restricts a lender from calling a loan due when a residence is transferred into a revocable trust in which the borrower is a beneficiary. That protection is written around trusts and does not extend the same way to a transfer into an LLC. Lenders frequently consent when asked, but the sequence matters: asking first is a different conversation than recording first and asking later.

Is Colorado as good as Wyoming for holding real estate in an entity?

For the things Wyoming is marketed on, no, and it is worth being straight about that. Wyoming's pitch is built on anonymity and charging order protection. Colorado's advantages are different and more modest: forming and maintaining an LLC here is inexpensive, the annual periodic report fee is 25 dollars, and there is no franchise tax. The more important point is that Colorado real property is governed by Colorado law and sits in a Colorado county's records regardless of where the entity was formed, so forming in another state does not move the property there.

Do I need a Colorado LLC, or can I use one formed in another state?

An entity formed elsewhere that holds Colorado real property will generally need to register to do business in Colorado, which means a Colorado registered agent and the same annual filing, so the paperwork does not disappear by forming out of state. Whether a Colorado entity or a foreign one fits better is a question for your attorney, and the answer usually turns on where the rest of your holdings are rather than on anything about the property itself.

Does an LLC protect me if someone is injured at my Vail property?

It can limit which assets are exposed, which is not the same as making you untouchable. An entity separates the property from your other assets if it is genuinely maintained as a separate entity, with its own account, its own records, and no mixing of personal and entity money. Where owners get into trouble is treating the LLC as a name on a deed rather than as a business. It also does not replace insurance. Owners who rent a property out are usually carrying liability coverage written for that use regardless of how title is held.

What does it cost to hold Vail Valley property in an LLC?

The direct costs are small and the indirect ones are easier to underestimate. Formation is inexpensive in Colorado and the annual periodic report is 25 dollars. Beyond that come the costs owners tend not to price in: a separate bank account and bookkeeping, a registered agent if you are not local, possible additional accounting work, a lender conversation if there is a mortgage, and potentially a transfer tax on the transfer itself depending on the jurisdiction. Whether that adds up to a reasonable price for the benefit is exactly the conversation to have with your advisors before rather than after.

Does holding property in a trust avoid estate tax?

A revocable trust does not, and this is the most common misunderstanding. A revocable trust avoids probate, which is a process, not a tax. The property remains in your estate for tax purposes because you kept control of it. Irrevocable structures can affect estate tax treatment, but they involve genuinely giving up control, which is a significant decision and firmly in your attorney's territory rather than mine.

When is the right time to set this up, before or after buying?

Structuring before a purchase is generally simpler than restructuring after one, because a later transfer is its own recorded conveyance and carries whatever consequences that brings, including the transfer tax question and the lender question. That said, plenty of owners set this up years into ownership and it is a normal thing to do. The reason to raise it early is that it is cheaper to plan than to unwind.
