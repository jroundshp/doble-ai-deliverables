A correction worth carrying

The DMR page everybody lands on first, dmr.nd.gov/oilgas/stats/statisticsvw.asp, is production data only. Barrels and mcf by month, county and formation, back to 1980. It publishes no well counts, no permits, no inactive wells, no plugging and no reclamation figures.

That is the wrong page for this business, and it is the one a search will hand you. The numbers that matter to Concrete Warrior are on the two DMR pages above.

Demand driver 01

Reclamation topsoil

This is the lead product, and it is the one with a regulation behind it. A well site is not released from its bond until the topsoil has been respread and native vegetation is established. That makes reclamation a compliance cost rather than discretionary spend, which is a materially better thing to sell into.

What the rule actually requires, in order

  1. Remove equipment, waste and debris from the site.
  2. Remove the gravel or surfacing material. This is a service we can sell alongside the topsoil, on the same trip.
  3. Remediate stabilized soil.
  4. Distribute the stockpiled topsoil evenly.
  5. Revegetate with native species.
The honest caveat on 2,160

The rule says stockpiled topsoil. The operator is expected to have salvaged the site's own soil, eight inches deep, when the pad was built. So our served market is not all 2,160 sites. It is the share where the stockpile is short, was lost, was contaminated, or was never properly salvaged, plus the sites where an operator would rather buy screened material than sort through what is on hand.

2,160 is the denominator, not our addressable market. It should not be quoted as ours in a pitch or on the website.

There is a second thing in here worth naming. A buyer pays for screened material rather than scraping together what is on site because the bond release depends on the vegetation taking. That is the argument the trommel photograph makes better than any sentence.

The delivery radius

Ninety minutes from New Town

New Town sits in Mountrail County, on the Fort Berthold Reservation. McKenzie, Dunn, McLean, Ward and Williams are all inside a reasonable haul. The top five producing counties in the state are McKenzie, Williams, Dunn, Mountrail and Divide, so four of the five are inside or adjacent to that radius.

Not on the list, and central

The MHA Nation. Fort Berthold is tribal land. The Nation is a buyer in its own right on roads and infrastructure, and it sits between us and every operator on the reservation. Being based in New Town is the advantage there, and no competitor list or operator ranking captures it.

The gap worth closing next

The operator list is missing the reclamation and plugging contractors who do the physical work. They are the ones who actually order topsoil by the load. An operator sets the budget; a contractor places the order.

We do not have their names yet. NDIC contract awards are where they are, and that is the next research job on this side.

Limits

What this does not tell us

This sizes the market around Concrete Warrior. It does not close the volume question, which still needs your numbers rather than the state's: yards produced per week, what a typical reclamation order looks like, and how far we will actually haul. The website carries that as a marked gap and this data does not fill it.

There is also a limit on the state side. DMR does not publish inactive or abandoned well counts by county on any public page found here. Getting the Mountrail and McKenzie slice of the 2,160 would mean asking DMR directly on (701) 328-8020, or working through the NorthSTAR system and the GIS Map Server. That is worth doing before anyone builds a forecast on the county-level number, because right now the 2,160 is only available statewide.

What came out of it

The conclusion this data produced

Laying the state calendar against the current plan produced one finding worth more than any single figure above: native grass has only two seeding windows, before 24 May or after 20 October, and the NDDOT spring load restrictions cover most of the first one. That leaves the fall as the real delivery season for reclamation topsoil, and it opens the week the operations memo shuts North Dakota down.

That argument, the three options it produces, and the questions only you and Richard can answer are in the winter brief, sent 29 August.
