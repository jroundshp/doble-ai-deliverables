A correction worth carrying

The DMR page everybody lands on first, dmr.nd.gov/oilgas/stats/statisticsvw.asp, is production data only. Barrels and mcf by month, county and formation, back to 1980. It publishes no well counts, no permits, no inactive wells, no plugging and no reclamation figures.

That is the wrong page for this business, and it is the one a search will hand you. The numbers that matter to Concrete Warrior are in the Director's Cut and on the reclamation page.

The finding this produces

Almost everything that matters is inside 50 miles. Watford City sits 39 miles out, and it is the seat of McKenzie County, which alone is about a third of all North Dakota oil production. Killdeer in Dunn County is 44. Tioga and Ray in Williams are 35 and 40.

The state's top five oil counties are McKenzie, Williams, Dunn, Mountrail and Divide, and they make up roughly 96.5% of production. Four of those five are in this circle, and one of them is home. The gap in the middle of the Bakken is where the yard already is.

3,040 active wells is the reclamation pipeline in our own backyard. It does not arrive in one season. It arrives over years as those wells age out, which is the right way to describe it to anyone who asks how durable this is.

2,333 of those 3,040 sit on Trust land. That is the most useful denominator on this page, because it is specific to the ground we are already on rather than to the state. It carries a condition worth knowing before anyone quotes a timeline: work on Trust land needs DMR, the MHA Nation and the BIA, where fee land needs only the first. Three approvals move slower than one.

One number cuts the other way and belongs here rather than in a footnote. Fort Berthold ran a single rig in June. New pad construction inside our own circle is close to nothing right now, so the near-term case rests on reclamation and on maintaining what already exists, not on new drilling.

Not a number, and central

The MHA Nation. Fort Berthold is tribal land. The Nation is a buyer in its own right on roads and infrastructure, and it sits between us and every operator on the reservation. Being based in New Town is the advantage here, and no competitor list or state table captures it.

The lead product

Reclamation topsoil

This is the product with a regulation behind it. A well site is not released from its bond until the topsoil has been respread and native vegetation is established, which makes reclamation a compliance cost rather than discretionary spend. That is a materially better thing to sell into.

Locally the denominator is the 2,333 Trust-land wells above, plus the 707 on fee land. The statewide figures below are context for how big and how funded this work is in general, not a measure of what is available to us.

What the rule actually requires, in order

  1. Remove equipment, waste and debris from the site.
  2. Remove the gravel or surfacing material. This is a service we can sell alongside the topsoil, on the same trip.
  3. Remediate stabilized soil.
  4. Distribute the stockpiled topsoil evenly.
  5. Revegetate with native species.
The honest caveat

The rule says stockpiled topsoil. The operator is expected to have salvaged the site's own soil, eight inches deep, when the pad was built. So our served market is not every well site. It is the share where the stockpile is short, was lost, was contaminated, or was never properly salvaged, plus the sites where an operator would rather buy screened material than sort through what is on hand.

Every well count on this page is a denominator, not our addressable market. None of them should be quoted as ours in a pitch or on the website.

There is a second thing in here worth naming. A buyer pays for screened material rather than scraping together what is on site because the bond release depends on the vegetation taking. That is the argument the trommel photograph makes better than any sentence.

The gap worth closing next

This list is missing the reclamation and plugging contractors who do the physical work. They are the ones who actually order topsoil by the load. An operator sets the budget, a contractor places the order.

We do not have their names yet. NDIC contract awards are where they are, and that is the next research job on this side.

Roughly 90 permits and 75 completions a month statewide is the pipeline of new pad work. That is modest and steady. It is not a boom, and nothing in this data should be used to argue that it is one. Against that, Fort Berthold is running one rig, so our own share of the new-pad work is thinner than the state number suggests.

Permits are the part worth watching rather than production, because operators pull them 90 to 120 days before they drill. The Director's Cut publishes that trend every month with the county detail behind it, which makes it the closest thing available to a forward look at where pad work is going to land.

Limits

What this does not tell us

The biggest limit is the one that matters most for a radius view. DMR publishes county-level production only as charts, not as tables, and its rig statistics workbook stops in 2016. So the current county-by-county numbers inside our circle cannot be pulled from the public files, only the Fort Berthold page and the statewide totals. Anything more granular means asking DMR directly.

It also does not close the volume question, which needs your numbers rather than the state's: yards produced per week, what a typical reclamation order looks like, and how far we will actually haul. The website carries that as a marked gap and this data does not fill it.

What came out of it

The conclusion this data produced

Laying the state calendar against the current plan produced one finding worth more than any single figure above: native grass has only two seeding windows, before 24 May or after 20 October, and the NDDOT spring load restrictions cover most of the first one. That leaves the fall as the real delivery season for reclamation topsoil, and it opens the week the operations memo shuts North Dakota down.

That argument, the three options it produces, and the questions only you and Richard can answer are in the winter brief, sent 29 August.
