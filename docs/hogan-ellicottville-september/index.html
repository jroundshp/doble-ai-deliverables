01
Findability

Findability is whether a search engine can reach a page at all, and whether it lands on one version of it.

Alpine was quietly running three copies of itself. The site answered on the plain address, the www address and the insecure address as three separate live sites rather than forwarding between them. Search signals were splitting three ways and nothing on the surface showed it. All three now forward to one address. verified by render

Two Bake Shop pages had been set to hide from search, which was recommendation 02 in August. They are visible again, and there is now no hide-from-search instruction anywhere on the site. The robots.txt line that Google ignores but Bing obeys is also gone. verified by render

Both sites now serve a correct, self-referencing canonical on every page, so no page is competing with a duplicate of itself. verified by render

02
Searchability

Searchability is whether a machine reading the page can tell what the business is, where it is, when it is open and what it sells. This is the layer that decides whether an AI assistant can answer a question about you, and it is where the largest single move happened on each site.

The Bake Shop's structured data went from generic to complete. August graded this the lowest thing on the site: present but with no bakery type, no address, no hours, no phone. The homepage, About and Menu now each carry a Bakery record with a postal address, an opening-hours specification, an organization and a breadcrumb trail, and About and Menu are additionally typed for what they are. verified by render

Alpine's structured data was worse than absent, it was contradictory. Two blocks on the same page disagreed about the hours and the address, which is the one failure mode that actively misleads rather than merely leaving a gap. They agree now, on every page. On top of that, the ten questions on the About page are marked up as an FAQ, which is what allows an answer to be lifted directly into a search result or an assistant's reply, and the menu link points at the menu rather than the About page. verified by render

Alpine also had no social preview image on five of its six pages, so sharing the menu or the event page to Facebook or a text message produced a bare link with no picture. All six now carry one. verified by render

03
Accuracy, and the hours
The hours now agree on five surfaces

Sunday through Thursday 8 AM to 4 PM, Friday 8 to 8, Saturday 8 to 9. That now reads the same in the footer of every page on alpinebistrony.com, in the data search engines read from those pages, on ellicottvillecoffee.com, on the Alpine Bistro Google profile and on the Ellicottville Coffee Company Google profile. verified by render

The coffee site was the one place they disagreed, and it was wrong on all seven days: it told customers the place stayed open an hour later Sunday through Thursday, opened an hour short on Friday and closed two hours early on Saturday. It runs on Square with a separate login, so this one was not ours to fix, and it was corrected this week by the person who holds it. The correction carried through to the Google profile attached to that site, which we checked a day at a time rather than reading the single row Google displays for today. verified by render

Three other accuracy problems on the coffee site are fixed. The homepage no longer claims the beans are roasted in house. The founding story now reads as history rather than as a description of who runs the place today. And an offer of guided fly fishing and snowshoe trips, with no way to book them anywhere on the site, has been removed. verified by render

On Alpine, the phone number and the price range are now in the formats Google expects, a wine was listed twice on the bar page and now appears once, and the same in-house roasting claim was removed from the five places it appeared. verified by render

One small thing still sitting in the coffee site

The Location and Hours block still carries a second phone number, (716) 217-4047, inside its stored content. Nobody sees it, because Square displays the synced business number over the top of it. It would become visible if that sync were ever switched off. verified by render

04
Cleanup

Alpine's images went from 25.9 MB to 3.5 MB, an 87% cut, with the event page alone dropping from 13.3 MB to 1.6 MB. On ski-town mobile signal this was the slowest thing either business owned, and it was the page selling the highest-margin drinks. verified by render

Pinch-to-zoom works again on phones. The theme had been blocking it, which is a real accessibility problem on a menu page and an annoyance everywhere else. verified by render

The padlock warning is gone from every Alpine page. The cause turned out to sit deeper than the logo setting everyone suspected: WordPress had the site's own address stored insecurely and was handing out image paths to match. Fixing it at that root also created the forwarding described in section 01. Fifty image references moved to secure addresses, and there are now zero insecure references on the site. verified by render

Every content image on Alpine now has alt text, for screen readers and for image search, and the event page no longer has two headings competing to say what the page is about. verified by render

One thing worth stating plainly before the next section. Most of what is in this update is invisible to somebody standing on Washington Street. Image weight, insecure references and duplicate hostnames are all real and all worth fixing, and none of them puts a person in a chair. The items in the next section are the ones that can.

05
What we could work on next
The biggest one: the two businesses barely point at each other

Alpine links to the Bake Shop from all six of its pages. The coffee site links to Alpine three times on its homepage and names it six times. The Bake Shop links to Alpine zero times, and the word "Alpine" does not appear on its homepage, About, Menu, Contact, Catering or Employment page at all. re-verified 11 September

So the traffic flows one way. Two of the three sites feed Alpine, and the site with the strongest content in the group, a block away, with its own customer base and its own Google profile, sends nothing. The same asymmetry shows up in the two Google profiles: Ellicottville Coffee Company carries 163 reviews to Alpine Bistro's 74, at the same address and phone number.

Cross-promotion here is not a marketing campaign, it is a handful of honest links and a paragraph on each site saying what the other one is. It is the cheapest item on this list and the only one that moves customers rather than rankings.

01
Link the three businesses to each other, deliberately Both
Start with the Bake Shop, which currently mentions Alpine nowhere. A line on the About page and a link in the footer, pointing at a café and bar run by the same family a block away, is worth more to a visiting skier than any technical item on this list. Then decide whether the coffee site should point at the Bake Shop too, which today it does not.
02

03

The dishes and prices are already real text on the page rather than a PDF or a photograph, which is the hard part and which most restaurant sites get wrong. Marking them up as a menu is what lets an assistant answer what Alpine serves and what it costs. Nothing new has to be written.

Neither site has a Bing verification tag or file. ChatGPT's search runs on Bing's index, so this is the cheapest single thing either business can do for AI visibility. Ten minutes each.

Home, menu, bar and event sit between 316 and 344 words each. Now that the technical work is done this is the ceiling, and it is the slowest item here because it is genuinely writing rather than configuration.

This is a business decision rather than a website job, which is why it is last rather than because it matters least. 163 reviews on one profile and 74 on the other is a real asset either way, and the wrong move burns it. Merging preserves review equity, deleting destroys it. It deserves its own conversation regardless of who does the building. our reading, not a finding
06
Check it yourselves

Set each engine up properly, because "use a private window" is only correct for two of the four.

Google and Perplexity: an incognito window is the right control. It drops your account and your search history. On Google it will not change your location, which it reads from your internet connection, so results still reflect where the browser physically is.

ChatGPT: incognito will not do it. ChatGPT's Memory is attached to the account rather than the browser, it is switched on by default, and a private window does not touch it. Use Temporary Chat instead, the mode that ignores memory and history entirely. If you are signed in, also open Settings and read what Memory has stored about the business, which is worth knowing in its own right.

Grok: it requires a sign-in, so incognito only produces a login screen. Sign in and start a brand new conversation rather than continuing an existing thread, since an open thread carries its own context.

Have five different people run it, not one. These systems are not deterministic. The same question asked twice can name different businesses, so one person running it once is a sample of one and can mislead in either direction. Five people means five different accounts, histories and connections, and what comes back is the range rather than a single reading.

Keep it comparable. Use the prompts word for word, since rephrasing changes the answer. Same order, same day. Screenshot every answer, including the sources or citations panel where the engine shows one, because who gets cited matters as much as who gets named. Put everything in one shared folder labelled by person and engine. If everyone is on the same office connection, Google sees one location for all of them, which is fine as long as you know it.

Re-run the identical set monthly. The movement in those answers is the scoreboard for the rest of the work.
