01 Objective
The objective, stated plainly

The website, the Google Business Profile and every other digital asset built here exist for one purpose: to be a sales tool.

A place a decision maker understands who this company is, sees what it sells and to what specification, sees the credentials that prove it can be trusted with the work, and comes away confident enough to buy. Everything here is downstream of that. If something does not help a buyer understand the company, evaluate the material or gain confidence, it does not belong in the build. This is not a brochure and not a branding exercise. It is what does the selling when nobody is on the phone, which in this trade is most of the time.

The point the whole build turns on: the credibility already exists. It is simply not visible. This is not a startup. The name has history here, the operation has been running, and the people involved hold established relationships on Fort Berthold and with operators in the basin. What is new is the phase, not the business.

So the job is not to manufacture credibility. It is to make credibility that already exists legible to a buyer who has not met these people. Someone who has worked with them already knows what they can do. A dispatcher three counties away, a procurement officer comparing suppliers on paper, and an assistant answering "who supplies concrete near New Town" get nothing at all today. Published specifications, published prices, named credentials, real photographs of real work, and answers with enough depth that a reader concludes these people know the trade. Depth reads as substantial to this buyer. Breadth reads as scattered.

Concrete Warrior Supply operates a ready mix concrete batch plant and an aggregate operation at 9702 ND-23, New Town, North Dakota, on the Fort Berthold Reservation. The legal entity is Concrete Warrior LLC, a licensed contractor with the MHA Nation. It sells ready mix concrete, crushed concrete, road gravel and topsoil into both tribal and non-tribal markets.

Who the buyer is
Not homeowners. Project managers, dispatchers, excavation and site contractors, oil field service companies, tribal programs and public works. Often standing on a job site, on a phone, with poor signal.
What they want to know
Three things, in this order: do you have the material, can you get it to my site this week, and what does it cost. Every layout decision should serve that sequence.
The lead product
Reclamation topsoil. Revised August 24, 2026, replacing ready mix concrete. Topsoil is the volume, the revenue and the reason most buyers call. Concrete is the second line and holds a genuine structural advantage of its own, set out in section 03. Aggregate, scoria, crushing and recycling follow behind both.
Definition of success
A contractor within delivery range who needs concrete or aggregate finds this business, gets a real answer including a price range, believes it, and calls. Measured by calls and quote requests, not by traffic.
What actually sells topsoil, established August 24, 2026

The buyer is not choosing on soil quality. They are choosing on what it costs to get it to their site.

Reclamation topsoil moves in jobs of roughly eight to ten thousand yards. At that volume freight is not a line item, it is most of the landed cost. Material produced inside the basin removes that haul, and the delivered saving runs to multiples of what the material itself costs at the gate.

That turns the central argument from a claim about the material into arithmetic the buyer can check, which is a far stronger position because it asks nobody to take a supplier's word for anything. It also means the highest value interactive element on this site is not a contact form. It is a delivered cost comparison, specified in section 03.

Concrete keeps its own argument, untouched. Concrete cannot be bought from far away because it goes off in transit. Topsoil can be, but at these volumes it should not be. Two different reasons to buy locally, and the site makes both separately rather than blurring them into one claim about proximity.

The single most important constraint

This is a market where almost no competitor publishes anything useful. The advantage available here is specificity: real material specs, real price brackets, real photographs, real answers to real questions. A handsome site with vague copy loses to a plain site with numbers on it.

Read that as licence to spend the effort on content structure rather than on visual novelty. A plain page carrying real numbers beats a handsome one carrying adjectives, and this market has no shortage of the second kind.

03 Site Structure

Roughly 24 pages at launch. The rule throughout: one page per thing somebody searches for. A single page trying to cover nine materials ranks for none of them.

Product lines confirmed August 12, 2026 — and topsoil splits in two

The business sells ready mix concrete, crushed concrete, road gravel and topsoil, into both tribal and non-tribal markets.

Topsoil needs two pages, not one. It is the same material out of the same pit, but it is two different sales. An oil and gas operator is buying proof the soil is not brine damaged and wants lab numbers: electrical conductivity, sodium adsorption ratio, pH, organic matter. A tribal, federal or public bid buyer is buying a county inspector's weed free certificate under N.D.C.C. 4.1-47. Different numbers, different vocabulary, different search terms. One page attempting both does neither.

Crushed concrete is not road gravel and gets its own page and its own argument. The batch plant generates returned loads and washout, which are feedstock, so the material comes out of the operation's own waste stream. It is cheaper than virgin aggregate for the buyer and nobody else in this market is marketing it.

New page, added v3: concrete disposal. The best content opportunity on this site.

The operation accepts clean concrete dropped at the yard, well under what a landfill charges to take it. That is a page, not a paragraph.

It is the only page that sells to the buyer rather than buying from them. A contractor tearing out slab has a disposal problem and a disposal budget, searches in those words, and today finds a landfill. Own search intent, own audience, and a published price carrying none of the sensitivity attached to material pricing. It also feeds the crusher, so what arrives is feedstock rather than cost. None of the twenty six supplier sites reviewed has one.

Requirements: the price, the landfill comparison, what counts as clean and what gets turned away, yard hours, directions. [TBC — landfill gate rates at New Town and Watford City]

Two required elements on the reclamation topsoil page, added v3

1. A delivered cost comparison, not a quantity calculator. The generic tool returns tonnage, which is worth little because the buyer already knows roughly what the job takes. What they do not know is what it costs delivered from here against what they pay now.

So it takes job size and distance, returns material plus freight to their site, then asks for the landed price they are paying today and shows the difference. Having the buyer supply that number matters twice over: it is more persuasive because it is theirs rather than a supplier's claim, and it keeps this site from publishing an assertion about a named competitor's pricing that we would then have to stand behind. No competitor site reviewed, locally or nationally, returns a delivered price at all.

2. A capacity block. Daily screening capacity, typical job size, and the number that converts: how long it takes to fill a ten thousand yard site. The real anxiety on a reclamation job is schedule, not price, because the closure date is fixed and a supplier who cannot keep up costs more than a higher unit price does. Capacity is throughput rather than margin, so it is safe to publish, and nobody publishes it. [TBC — the figure the operating side will state publicly]

Revised v3: why topsoil leads, and why concrete's argument survives intact

Topsoil leads because it is the volume and the revenue, and because the field is effectively open. Demand is driven by well site reclamation, which runs at roughly ten thousand cubic yards a site, plus yard and development work and state and municipal right of way. No supplier in this region markets it seriously and none publishes anything about it.

Concrete is a different kind of position and the site should make its case separately. West Dakota Ready Mix in New Town is permanently closed. The nearest operating plants are Tioga at roughly sixty miles and Dickinson and Bowman at 150 to 200. Concrete has a placement window of about ninety minutes, which makes distance decisive in a way it never is for gravel. A plant at New Town is therefore the only practical option for most of Fort Berthold. That is a position built on geography, and no competitor answers it without building a plant.

The same reading holds from a second direction: on the published MHA TERO preference lists there is no concrete or ready mix category at any level. Aggregate, reclamation, trucking, erosion control and road and pad construction are all represented. Concrete is absent entirely.

The two lines therefore need two postures, and the pages should not sound alike. On concrete this operation is entering a market that Knife River holds, having bought out Strata, and the argument is that distance makes the incumbent impractical for most of Fort Berthold. On topsoil there is no incumbent to displace and the argument is arithmetic. Writing both pages in the same challenger voice would waste the stronger of the two positions.

On service area pages

Build them only for towns genuinely served, and write each one differently. Thin near duplicate pages generated for every town within a hundred miles are treated as doorway pages, get filtered, and can drag down the rest of the site. Four real pages beat thirty generated ones.

Concrete and aggregate have different radii and the pages should say so. Concrete is limited by the placement window; aggregate is limited by freight cost.

05 Content, Photography And Tone

Photography

Real photographs of this operation. No stock imagery and no AI generated images. That is the finished state and it is a firm requirement rather than a preference. v3 permits one narrow interim exception on the way there, set out immediately below.

The client will supply roughly thirty daylight phone photographs of the plant, the pit, stockpiles, trucks, loading and finished pours. Slightly rough, real photographs of this yard outperform polished stock photography in this trade, because they are the one thing a competitor cannot copy and a template cannot fake. They get cropped, compressed and sized properly. They do not get replaced with something tidier.

Revised v3, August 24, 2026: photography no longer holds the launch

v2 held the launch until every photograph was of this operation, which made the shoot a gate on the whole build. That is relaxed on the client's decision. The site launches on photographs from the affiliated Texas operation plus whatever usable frames have come back from New Town, and images are replaced as the drone and professional shoot deliver. The swap list is a live document and the swaps are weeks away, not months.

The relaxation is narrower than it sounds, and the limits are the point:

  • Borrowed frames may carry atmosphere. They may not carry identity. Iron working, a loader, trucks in motion: acceptable as interim. A stockpile presented as a specific product, anything a reader would take as the New Town yard, anything captioned as this operation: not acceptable at any point. The whole argument of this site is that the material is produced where the buyer is building, and an image that quietly contradicts it does more damage than an empty space.
  • Some slots stay empty until the shoot. There is no scoria in Texas and the red colour is the product's whole identity. Class 5 and Class 13 are distinguished by gradation, so a photograph of the wrong rock is not a placeholder, it is a false spec. The benchmark review found borrowed and stock imagery reads as broker rather than producer, which is exactly the impression to avoid.
  • Every interim image is logged with its swap target, so no borrowed frame quietly becomes permanent. That is the ordinary failure mode here.

One question settles first, and it is question 5 in section 08. The Texas stills carry the marks of a stock library rather than photographs of that yard: uniform dimensions, studio lighting, cloudless skies, no branding or signage in any frame. One confirmed background image there is a theme demo asset showing an excavator with a palm tree and a city skyline. Owning the affiliated company is not the same as holding the web vendor's stock licence, so someone has to confirm which of these were actually taken by or for the operation.

Shot list, in priority order

  • Wide establishing shot of the yard — the homepage image
  • The crusher running, with material moving. A crusher sitting idle is worth a fraction of one working
  • The batch plant, and a mixer truck discharging on a pour
  • Separate stockpiles, one clearly identifiable per product. Scoria especially, since it is red and instantly distinguishable
  • The trommel screening topsoil, and screened material placed on a reclamation site if one exists
  • The feedstock pile of broken slab and returns — this is the recycling argument in a single image

Three checks before any photograph is published

1. Location. Every image on the finished site must be this operation. Photographs from the affiliated Texas yard misrepresent the yard and undercut the point of the site being unambiguously about one place. See the v3 revision above for the one narrow interim exception and its limits, which permit borrowed frames to carry atmosphere but never identity, and never past the swap.

2. Branding on the equipment. Check truck doors, hard hats and signage in every frame. Equipment that arrived from the affiliated operation and still carries its livery puts another company's brand on this one's website. Reframe, retake or leave it out.

3. Safety. Anything showing a person without a hard hat or in an unsafe position does not go up. Buyers running ISNetworld and MSHA Part 46 compliance read photographs as evidence of how a site is operated.

Every image needs its location, approximate date and photographer recorded when it is handed over. That context cannot be reconstructed afterwards, and nothing gets captioned by guesswork.

Tone

  • Plain, specific, trade literate. Numbers and specs over adjectives.
  • No filler superlatives. "Premier provider of quality solutions" is worse than "Class 5, $14 a ton, delivered within 40 miles."
  • Do not overstate capability or capacity. Everything published must be defensible to a contractor who will notice.
Three copy rules that are not stylistic, and are not negotiable

1. Describe experience, not corporate structure. The experience the Texas crews bring north is real and worth stating, as capability: "our crews have been crushing, recycling and hauling aggregate in Texas since [year]." Not as structure: "the North Dakota division of a Texas company." Same underlying fact, and only the second causes a problem. No shared navigation, no combined locations page, no shared logo, no visual twinning between the two sites. If they look like siblings, the design has made the structural claim the copy carefully avoided.

2. No claim about ownership or certification until it is granted in writing. TERO certification questions are open. Nothing on the site, the Google listing or any profile asserts a preference status, certification or ownership characteristic that has not been formally issued, and when one is granted the wording is the credential's exact wording and nothing broader. This is the one error on the project that would be genuinely hard to recover from, because the audience for the claim is also the body that verifies it.

3. Name no customer, operator or partner without written permission. A named major operator carries more weight than any amount of copy, which is exactly why it cannot go up on an assumption that it would be fine. Most operators have rules about supplier use of their name and a supplier who breaches one does not get asked again. Until permission is in hand, describe the work rather than the client: the scale, the material, the spec, the conditions. Publishable, and more useful to a reader deciding whether these people can handle a job like theirs.
