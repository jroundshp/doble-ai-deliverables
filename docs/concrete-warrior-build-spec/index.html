01 Objective
The objective, stated plainly

The website, the Google Business Profile and every other digital asset built here exist for one purpose: to be a sales tool.

A place a decision maker can go to understand who this company is, see exactly what it sells and to what specification, see the certifications and credentials that prove it can be trusted with the work, and come away confident enough to buy. Every requirement in this document is downstream of that. If something on the site does not help a buyer understand the company, evaluate the material, or gain confidence, it does not belong in the build.

Two things follow from stating it that way. First, this is not a brochure and it is not a branding exercise — it is the thing that has to do the selling when nobody is on the phone, which in this trade is most of the time.

Second, and this is the point the whole build turns on: the credibility already exists. It is simply not visible. This is not a startup. The name has history in this area, the operation has been running, and the people involved hold established working relationships on Fort Berthold and with operators in the basin. What is new is the phase, not the business — new people involved, a deliberate move onto a professional footing, substantial upgrades to the operation, and for the first time a real digital presence to match. This project is the next phase being taken seriously, and the site has to look like a company that is taking itself seriously.

So the site's job is not to manufacture credibility out of nothing. It is to make credibility that already exists legible to a buyer who has not met these people. Someone who has worked with them already knows what they can do. A dispatcher three counties away, a procurement officer comparing suppliers on paper, and an AI assistant answering "who supplies concrete near New Town" get nothing at all today. Everything specified in this document exists to close that gap: published specifications, published prices, named credentials, real photographs of real equipment doing real work, and answers written with enough depth that a reader concludes these people know the trade. Depth reads as substantial to this buyer. Breadth reads as scattered.

Concrete Warrior Supply operates a ready mix concrete batch plant and an aggregate operation at 9702 ND-23, New Town, North Dakota, on the Fort Berthold Reservation. The legal entity is Concrete Warrior LLC, a licensed contractor with the MHA Nation. It sells ready mix concrete, crushed concrete, road gravel and topsoil into both tribal and non-tribal markets.

Who the buyer is
Not homeowners. Project managers, dispatchers, excavation and site contractors, oil field service companies, tribal programs and public works. Often standing on a job site, on a phone, with poor signal.
What they want to know
Three things, in this order: do you have the material, can you get it to my site this week, and what does it cost. Every layout decision should serve that sequence.
The lead product
Reclamation topsoil. Revised August 24, 2026, replacing ready mix concrete. Topsoil is the volume, the revenue and the reason most buyers call. Concrete is the second line and holds a genuine structural advantage of its own, set out in section 03. Aggregate, scoria, crushing and recycling follow behind both.
Definition of success
A contractor within delivery range who needs concrete or aggregate finds this business, gets a real answer including a price range, believes it, and calls. Measured by calls and quote requests, not by traffic.
What actually sells topsoil, established August 24, 2026

The buyer is not choosing on soil quality. They are choosing on what it costs to get it to their site.

Reclamation topsoil moves in jobs of roughly eight to ten thousand yards. At that volume freight is not a line item, it is most of the landed cost. A buyer hauling material into the basin from outside it pays for every mile of it, on every load, and the delivered price ends up multiples of the price at the gate. Material produced inside the basin removes that haul.

This changes the site's central argument from a claim about the material to an arithmetic problem the buyer can check. That is a far stronger position, because it does not ask anyone to take a supplier's word for anything. It also means the highest value interactive element on this site is not a contact form. It is a delivered cost comparison, specified in section 03.

Concrete keeps its own argument and it is not weakened by any of this. Concrete cannot be bought from far away because it goes off in transit. Topsoil can be, but at these volumes it should not be. Perishability and freight are two different reasons to buy locally, and the site should make both, separately, rather than blurring them into one claim about proximity.

The single most important constraint

This is a market where almost no competitor publishes anything useful. The advantage available here is specificity: real material specs, real price brackets, real photographs, real answers to real questions. A handsome site with vague copy loses to a plain site with numbers on it.

Read that as licence to spend the effort on content structure rather than on visual novelty. A plain page carrying real numbers beats a handsome one carrying adjectives, and this market has no shortage of the second kind.

02 Name And Domain — Resolved
Decided August 13, 2026

Trading name: Concrete Warrior Supply.
Domain: concretewarriorsupply.com, verified available at the registry on August 13, 2026.

Legal entity remains Concrete Warrior LLC. The trading name adds one word; it does not change the filing, and the entity name is what goes on any TERO application.

Why Supply, and not the shorter name

  • The business sells four material lines and three of them are not concrete — crushed concrete, road gravel and topsoil alongside ready mix. A concrete-only name undersells three quarters of the catalogue, and it works hardest against topsoil, which is the least contested product off the reservation. Nobody searching for reclamation topsoil clicks a company called Concrete Warrior.
  • Nobody else in this market puts a material in the name. Ruach Resources, Knife River, Fisher Industries, Strata, Triple C. Aggregate businesses expand across materials and the name has to outlive the product mix.
  • It passes the truck door test. Three plain words, no spelling required on a phone call. A domain carrying a state abbreviation has to be spelled out every time it is spoken, and every spelling-out is a chance to lose the call.
  • The brand equity survives. Everyone still says Concrete Warrior. Supply signals a materials yard rather than a concrete contractor.

Two prerequisites that sit ahead of the build

1. Entity standing. The North Dakota Secretary of State registry currently returns Concrete Warrior LLC as Inactive – Involuntary, Not Good Standing, and the state's own portal shows the record eligible for reinstatement. In North Dakota that status almost always means a missed annual report rather than anything substantive. We could not retrieve the registered agent on the record to confirm it is this entity, because the Secretary of State's detail view is currently erroring for every record on the site, so this is worth one call to 701-328-2900 rather than an assumption. A dissolved entity complicates verifying a Google listing, signing contracts and holding a licence, so it wants settling first.

2. The Google Business Profile is the slowest item in the project. It asks for a website address during setup and verification on rural tribal land can take weeks and may need a second attempt. Register the domain and stand up a holding page with name, phone, address and service area before the site is built, so the listing can be filed against something. Standing rules

  • Registrant is the client. The domain is registered in the client's name, on the client's account, with the client holding the credentials. Never in a vendor's account.
  • Buy the obvious misspellings and redirect them. A few dollars a year, and it stops somebody else parking on them later.
  • One brand, not two. Trading under two names means two sites, two Google listings and two sets of local signals, roughly doubling the ongoing effort for no ranking benefit. This spec assumes one. See the open question below.
  • Name overlap to be aware of. A separate company, Warrior LLC, is an established contractor on Fort Berthold. It is not a competitor — it holds no aggregate or concrete categories — but the two names will sit near each other in front of the same people who award work. Worth raising directly with them rather than discovering later, and worth mentioning to TERO. It is a reason to keep the full three-word name in use consistently.
Open question for the client — Rhino Ready Mix

The Rhino name has been in the background of this project from the start and has never been settled, so it is captured here as a discussion point rather than left to surface later.

What the public record shows. Two North Dakota filings carry the name. RHINO READY MIX, LLC was filed 27 April 2018 out of Williston, and the registry now returns it as Inactive – Voluntary, meaning it was deliberately closed rather than lapsed. A second Rhino Ready Mix LLC was filed 23 December 2024 and shows Inactive – Involuntary. Neither carries a website, a Google listing, reviews, or motor carrier authority, and neither appears on any MHA TERO preference list. Separately, an unrelated and active Rhino Ready Mix operates out of Houston, Texas; it has held rhinoreadymix.com since 2015 and owns the search results for the name nationally.

The question. Is Rhino finished, or is it intended to come back? Nothing in this document depends on the answer being yes or no — it depends on the answer being known. If Rhino is retired, the path is simply Concrete Warrior Supply and there is nothing further to do. If it is meant to return, that is a second brand and a second set of listings and local signals, which is a real ongoing cost and should be a deliberate decision rather than a default. It would also mean permanently sharing a name and search results with an operating Texas ready mix company, which is awkward given the affiliated aggregate operation is also in Texas.

One practical item either way. Correspondence on this project has come from a Rhino-branded personal email address. If any customer-facing address is still on that domain or a personal account, it should move to the new domain at the same time it is registered — firstname@concretewarriorsupply.com. A quote arriving from one name while the website, the truck doors and the Google listing say another is the same inconsistency this build exists to remove, and it lands at the exact moment a buyer is deciding whether this is one company or a loose set of them.

Related, and worth settling in the same conversation. Three entities connected to this operation currently sit in a not-in-good-standing state at the Secretary of State, including Concrete Warrior LLC itself. That is paperwork rather than strategy, but a procurement officer at an operator who runs a routine entity check will see exactly what is written above. For a project whose stated purpose is putting the business on a professional footing, it is the item most at odds with the goal, and it is cheap to fix.

03 Site Structure

Roughly 24 pages at launch. The rule throughout: one page per thing somebody searches for. A single page trying to cover nine materials ranks for none of them.

Product lines confirmed August 12, 2026 — and topsoil splits in two

The business sells ready mix concrete, crushed concrete, road gravel and topsoil, into both tribal and non-tribal markets.

Topsoil needs two pages, not one. It is the same material out of the same pit, but it is two different sales. An oil and gas operator is buying proof the soil is not brine damaged and wants lab numbers: electrical conductivity, sodium adsorption ratio, pH, organic matter. A tribal, federal or public bid buyer is buying a county inspector's weed free certificate under N.D.C.C. 4.1-47. Different numbers, different vocabulary, different search terms. One page attempting both does neither.

Crushed concrete is not road gravel and gets its own page and its own argument. The batch plant generates returned loads and washout, which are feedstock, so the material comes out of the operation's own waste stream. It is cheaper than virgin aggregate for the buyer and nobody else in this market is marketing it.

New page, added v3: concrete disposal. The best content opportunity on this site.

The operation accepts clean concrete dropped at the yard, at a price well under what a landfill charges to take it. That is a page, not a paragraph, and it is unlike anything else specified here.

It is the only page on the site that sells to the buyer rather than buying from them. A contractor tearing out slab has a disposal problem and a disposal budget, searches for it in those words, and today finds a landfill. It has its own search intent, its own audience, and a published price that can go on the page without any of the sensitivity attached to material pricing. It also feeds the crusher, so the material arriving is feedstock rather than cost.

Across the twenty six supplier sites reviewed nationally for this project, not one publishes an inbound disposal page. Requirements: the price, the landfill comparison, what counts as clean concrete and what will be turned away, yard hours, and directions. Two required elements on the reclamation topsoil page, added v3

1. A delivered cost comparison, not a quantity calculator. The generic version of this tool returns tonnage. That is worth little, because the buyer already knows roughly how much material the job takes. What they do not know is what it costs delivered, from here, against what they are paying now.

So it takes the job size and the distance, returns the material and the freight to their site, and then asks the buyer for the landed price they are paying today and shows the difference. Having the buyer supply the comparison number is deliberate and it matters twice over: it is more persuasive because it is their own figure rather than a supplier's claim, and it keeps this site from publishing an assertion about a named competitor's pricing, which is a claim we would then have to stand behind. No competitor site reviewed for this project, locally or nationally, returns a delivered price at all.

2. A capacity block. Daily screening capacity, typical job size, and the number that actually converts: how long it takes to fill a ten thousand yard site. The buyer's real anxiety on a reclamation job is schedule rather than price, because a closure date is fixed and a supplier who cannot keep up costs them more than a higher unit price does. Capacity is throughput, not margin, so it is safe to publish, and no supplier reviewed for this project publishes it. [TBC — the figure the operating side is willing to state publicly]

Revised v3: why topsoil leads, and why concrete's argument survives intact

Topsoil leads because it is the volume and the revenue, and because the field is effectively open. Demand is driven by well site reclamation, which runs at roughly ten thousand cubic yards a site, plus yard and development work and state and municipal right of way. No supplier in this region markets it seriously and none publishes anything about it.

Concrete is a different kind of position and the site should make its case separately. West Dakota Ready Mix in New Town is permanently closed. The nearest operating plants are Tioga at roughly sixty miles and Dickinson and Bowman at 150 to 200. Concrete has a placement window of about ninety minutes, which makes distance decisive in a way it never is for gravel. A plant at New Town is therefore the only practical option for most of Fort Berthold. That is a position built on geography, and no competitor answers it without building a plant.

The same reading holds from a second direction: on the published MHA TERO preference lists there is no concrete or ready mix category at any level. Aggregate, reclamation, trucking, erosion control and road and pad construction are all represented. Concrete is absent entirely.

The two lines therefore need two postures, and the pages should not sound alike. On concrete this operation is entering a market that Knife River holds, having bought out Strata, and the argument is that distance makes the incumbent impractical for most of Fort Berthold. On topsoil there is no incumbent to displace and the argument is arithmetic. Writing both pages in the same challenger voice would waste the stronger of the two positions.

On service area pages

Build them only for towns genuinely served, and write each one differently. Thin near duplicate pages generated for every town within a hundred miles are treated as doorway pages, get filtered, and can drag down the rest of the site. Four real pages beat thirty generated ones.

Concrete and aggregate have different radii and the pages should say so. Concrete is limited by the placement window; aggregate is limited by freight cost.

05 Content, Photography And Tone

Photography

Real photographs of this operation. No stock imagery and no AI generated images. That is the finished state and it is a firm requirement rather than a preference. v3 permits one narrow interim exception on the way there, set out immediately below.

The client will supply roughly thirty daylight phone photographs of the plant, the pit, stockpiles, trucks, loading and finished pours. Slightly rough, real photographs of this yard outperform polished stock photography in this trade, because they are the one thing a competitor cannot copy and a template cannot fake. They get cropped, compressed and sized properly. They do not get replaced with something tidier.

Revised v3, August 24, 2026: photography no longer holds the launch

v2 required that every photograph be of this operation before anything went live, which made the shoot a gate on the whole build. That is now relaxed, deliberately, on the client's decision. The site launches on photographs from the affiliated Texas operation together with whatever usable frames have already come back from New Town, and images are replaced as the drone and professional shoot deliver. A photograph swap list is maintained as a live document, and the swaps are expected within weeks rather than months.

The relaxation is narrower than it sounds, and the limits are the point:

  • Borrowed frames may carry atmosphere. They may not carry identity. Iron working, a loader, a crusher, trucks in motion: acceptable as interim. A stockpile presented as a specific product, anything a reader would take as the New Town yard, and anything captioned as this operation: not acceptable at any point. The entire argument of this site is that the material is produced where the buyer is building, and an image that quietly contradicts it does more damage than an empty space would.
  • Some products have no stand in and their slots stay empty until the shoot. There is no scoria in Texas and the red colour is the whole of the product's identity. Class 5 and Class 13 are distinguished by gradation, so a photograph of the wrong rock is not a placeholder, it is a false spec.
  • An empty, labelled slot beats a misleading photograph. The benchmark review of twenty six supplier sites found borrowed and stock imagery reads as broker rather than producer, which is precisely the impression this business must avoid.
  • Every interim image is logged with its swap target so that no borrowed frame quietly becomes permanent, which is the ordinary failure mode here.

One question has to be settled before any of it goes public, and it is in section 08. The Texas stills carry the marks of a stock library rather than photographs of that yard: uniform dimensions, studio lighting, cloudless skies, no branding and no signage anywhere in frame. One confirmed background image on that site is a theme demo asset showing an excavator with a palm tree and a city skyline behind it. Owning the affiliated company is not the same as holding the web vendor's stock licence. Someone has to confirm which of those photographs were actually taken by or for the operation.

Shot list, in priority order

  • Wide establishing shot of the yard — the homepage image
  • The crusher running, with material moving. A crusher sitting idle is worth a fraction of one working
  • The batch plant, and a mixer truck discharging on a pour
  • Separate stockpiles, one clearly identifiable per product. Scoria especially, since it is red and instantly distinguishable
  • The trommel screening topsoil, and screened material placed on a reclamation site if one exists
  • The feedstock pile of broken slab and returns — this is the recycling argument in a single image
  • A loader filling a truck, and trucks arriving at a site. Delivery is most of what is actually being bought
  • Crews working, with their consent
  • Something with a person or a truck in frame for scale. Stockpiles photograph as beige lumps without it
Three checks before any photograph is published

1. Location. Every image on the finished site must be this operation. Photographs from the affiliated Texas yard misrepresent the yard and undercut the point of the site being unambiguously about one place. See the v3 revision above for the one narrow interim exception and its limits, which permit borrowed frames to carry atmosphere but never identity, and never past the swap.

2. Branding on the equipment. Check truck doors, hard hats and signage in every frame. Equipment that arrived from the affiliated operation and still carries its livery puts another company's brand on this one's website. Reframe, retake or leave it out.

3. Safety. Anything showing a person without a hard hat or in an unsafe position does not go up. Buyers running ISNetworld and MSHA Part 46 compliance read photographs as evidence of how a site is operated.

Every image needs its location, approximate date and photographer recorded when it is handed over. That context cannot be reconstructed afterwards, and nothing gets captioned by guesswork.

Tone

  • Plain, specific, trade literate. Numbers and specs over adjectives.
  • No filler superlatives. "Premier provider of quality solutions" is worse than "Class 5, $14 a ton, delivered within 40 miles."
  • Do not overstate capability or capacity. Everything published must be defensible to a contractor who will notice.
Three copy rules that are not stylistic, and are not negotiable

1. Describe experience, not corporate structure. There is an affiliated aggregate and recycling operation in Texas, and the experience its crews bring north is real and worth stating. State it as capability: "our crews have been crushing, recycling and hauling aggregate in Texas since [year]." Do not state it as structure: "the North Dakota division of a Texas company." Those describe the same underlying fact, and only the second one causes a problem. The relationship is a joint venture between separate companies and should be described that way, in prose, on the About page. No shared navigation, no combined "our locations" page, no shared logo or brand system, and no visual twinning between the two sites. If the two sites look like siblings, the design has made the structural claim that the copy carefully avoided.

2. Make no claim about ownership or certification status until it is granted in writing. Certification questions with MHA TERO are open at the time of writing. Nothing on the site, in the Google listing, or in any profile asserts a preference status, a certification or an ownership characteristic that has not been formally issued. When something is granted, the wording is the exact wording of the credential and nothing broader. Overstating this is the one error on this project that would be genuinely hard to recover from, because the audience for the claim is also the body that verifies it.

3. Name no customer, operator or partner without written permission. The relationships this operation holds are among its strongest assets, and a named major operator or tribal programme carries more weight than any amount of copy. That is exactly why it cannot be published on someone's assumption that it would be fine. Most operators have procurement and communications rules about supplier use of their name, and a supplier who breaches one does not get asked again. Until a permission is in hand, the same credibility is available without the risk: describe the work rather than the client — the scale, the material, the spec, the conditions — which is both publishable and more useful to a reader deciding whether these people can handle a job like theirs.
